    Quiz: Market Failures

    Test your understanding of externalities, public goods, information asymmetry, market power, and government responses to market failure with these review questions.

    1. What is a "market failure"?

    1. Any situation in which a firm earns zero economic profit in the long run
    2. A situation in which the free market, on its own, fails to allocate resources efficiently
    3. A government policy that reduces total surplus
    4. Any market where price is above marginal cost temporarily
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A market failure occurs whenever the free market fails to produce the surplus-maximizing (efficient) quantity on its own, typically because one of the conditions behind the First Welfare Theorem breaks down. Option A describes a normal, efficient long-run competitive outcome, option C describes government failure instead, and option D describes a feature of market power specifically, not market failure in general.

    Concept Tested: Market Failure

    2. What is an "externality"?

    1. A cost or benefit of a transaction that falls on a third party not involved in that transaction
    2. The difference between a firm's accounting profit and economic profit
    3. A tax imposed directly on the buyer or seller in a transaction
    4. The portion of consumer surplus captured by a monopolist
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. An externality exists when a transaction's costs or benefits spill onto someone who is not a party to that transaction and whose interests the market price does not reflect, such as pollution harming nearby residents. Option B describes a cost-accounting distinction unrelated to spillovers, option C describes a government policy tool rather than the market failure itself, and option D describes a transfer under market power, a separate category of failure.

    Concept Tested: Externality

    3. Which combination of criteria defines a "public good"?

    1. Excludable and rival
    2. Excludable and non-rival
    3. Non-excludable and rival
    4. Non-excludable and non-rival
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. A public good is both non-excludable (no one can be stopped from consuming it, even without paying) and non-rival (one person's use doesn't reduce what's available to others), as with national defense. Option A describes a private good, option B describes a club good, and option C describes a common-pool resource.

    Concept Tested: Public Good

    4. Why does a negative externality cause a market to overproduce relative to the efficient quantity?

    1. Because the private marginal cost curve lies below the true social marginal cost curve, so firms produce beyond the point where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit
    2. Because the private marginal benefit curve lies above the social marginal benefit curve
    3. Because negative externalities always eliminate consumer surplus entirely
    4. Because firms deliberately ignore their own private costs
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Since social marginal cost equals private marginal cost plus the external cost, a firm that ignores the external cost treats its (lower) private marginal cost as if it were the full cost, leading it to produce more than the socially efficient quantity. Option B misapplies the externality to the benefit side when the externality here is on the cost side, option C overstates the effect since consumer surplus is reduced, not eliminated, and option D mischaracterizes the firm as ignoring costs it actually does pay, rather than costs borne by third parties.

    Concept Tested: Negative Externality

    5. What is the key timing distinction between adverse selection and moral hazard?

    1. Adverse selection involves hidden information before a transaction, while moral hazard involves hidden behavior after a transaction
    2. Adverse selection occurs only in insurance markets, while moral hazard occurs only in labor markets
    3. Adverse selection involves government policy, while moral hazard involves private behavior
    4. There is no meaningful distinction; the two terms describe the same phenomenon
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Adverse selection concerns information hidden before a deal is struck (such as a used car's true quality), while moral hazard concerns behavior that changes after a deal is struck, once incentives shift (such as driving less carefully once insured). Option B wrongly restricts each concept to a single industry when both can appear across many markets, option C invents an unsupported government-versus-private distinction, and option D denies a well-established and meaningful difference.

    Concept Tested: Adverse Selection

    6. What causes the "tragedy of the commons" in a common-pool resource?

    1. Because the resource is excludable, allowing the owner to overcharge users
    2. Because each user bears only part of the cost of their own extraction, while the rest is spread across all other users, creating an incentive to overuse the resource
    3. Because the resource is non-rival, so no one has an incentive to use it at all
    4. Because government regulation always prevents any use of the resource
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Because a common-pool resource is rival but non-excludable, each user ignores the cost their own extraction imposes on others (a depleted shared resource), leading to systematic overuse even though restraint would benefit everyone collectively. Option A incorrectly describes the resource as excludable, which is the opposite of the defining condition, option C misapplies rivalry (common-pool resources are rival, not non-rival), and option D invents a regulatory claim not part of the basic mechanism.

    Concept Tested: Tragedy of the Commons

    7. A city grants an exclusive franchise for local internet service. Market demand is \(P = 90 - Q\), and marginal cost is constant at \(MC = 10\). What is the deadweight loss created by this market-power-based market failure?

    1. $400
    2. $800
    3. $1,600
    4. $3,200
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The competitive quantity is \(Q_C=80\) (where \(P=MC\)), and the monopoly quantity is \(Q_M=40\) (where \(MR=90-2Q=10\)), giving \(P_M=\$50\); the deadweight loss triangle is \(\frac{1}{2}\times(80-40)\times(50-10)=\frac{1}{2}\times40\times40=\$800\). The other options result from using only one of the two differences or from doubling instead of halving the triangle's area.

    Concept Tested: Market Power as Failure

    8. A city park is open to anyone, and no one can be charged for entering or stopped from enjoying it, while there is no limit on how many people can use it simultaneously without reducing enjoyment for others (within reason). How should this good be classified according to the excludability and rivalry criteria?

    1. Private good, since it is excludable and rival
    2. Club good, since it is excludable but non-rival
    3. Common-pool resource, since it is non-excludable but rival
    4. Public good, since it is non-excludable and non-rival
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The park cannot exclude non-payers and one visitor's enjoyment does not reduce another's (up to reasonable capacity), matching the non-excludable, non-rival definition of a public good. Option A and B both incorrectly describe the park as excludable, which it is not, and option C incorrectly describes it as rival, when the scenario specifies that use does not meaningfully reduce availability to others.

    Concept Tested: Public Good

    9. A firm holds an exclusive government license worth $900 per year in monopoly profit above the competitive benchmark. Based on the concept of rent-seeking, what is the most the firm would rationally be willing to spend lobbying to preserve this license?

    1. Nothing, since lobbying produces no new output
    2. Exactly $450, half of the monopoly profit
    3. Up to just under $900, the full value of the monopoly profit it is trying to protect
    4. An unlimited amount, since rent-seeking has no upper bound in theory
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Because the $900 is a prize created purely by restricting entry, a rational firm is willing to spend up to just under that full amount on lobbying, since doing so still leaves it better off than losing the license entirely. Option A ignores the real incentive firms have to protect valuable rents, option B invents an arbitrary fraction not implied by the rent-seeking logic, and option D is incorrect since spending more than the prize itself is worth would make the firm worse off than not lobbying at all.

    Concept Tested: Rent-Seeking

    10. A government imposes a price ceiling intending to correct a perceived market failure. Based on the chapter's discussion of government failure, under what condition would this intervention most likely make the problem worse rather than better?

    1. When the government has perfect information about the market and no political constraints
    2. When the policy exactly offsets an existing negative externality
    3. When the original market was already perfectly competitive and efficient, so the price ceiling creates a shortage where none existed before
    4. When the market failure being addressed is a public good problem rather than a price-related one
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. If the market was already efficient, imposing a price ceiling introduces a shortage where none existed, exactly the kind of unintended side effect the chapter identifies as government failure — the existence of high prices alone is not proof of a genuine market failure. Option A describes conditions under which intervention is more likely to succeed, not fail, option B describes a case where the policy is well-matched to an actual failure, and option D describes mismatching the tool to the wrong failure type, a different issue from the scenario described.

    Concept Tested: Government Failure
