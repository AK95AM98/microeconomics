    Quiz: International Trade

    Test your understanding of comparative advantage, the gains from trade, the Heckscher-Ohlin model, trade policy, and the balance of payments with these review questions.

    1. According to the Ricardian trade model, what determines which good a country has a comparative advantage in?

    1. Differences in labor productivity across countries, which determine relative opportunity costs
    2. Which good the country can produce in the largest absolute quantity
    3. The country's total population size
    4. Whether the country has a trade surplus or deficit
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. The Ricardian model explains comparative advantage through cross-country differences in labor productivity, which translate directly into differences in opportunity cost — the good a country can produce at a relatively lower opportunity cost is the good in which it holds a comparative advantage. Option B describes absolute advantage, a distinct and not decisive concept for trade, and options C and D introduce factors the model does not use to determine comparative advantage.

    Concept Tested: Ricardian Trade Model

    2. What is a "tariff"?

    1. A direct limit on the physical quantity of a good that may be imported
    2. A tax imposed by a government on imported goods, typically raising their price to domestic consumers
    3. A subsidy paid to domestic exporters
    4. A voluntary agreement between countries to eliminate all trade barriers
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. A tariff is a tax on imports that raises the domestic price of the good above the world price, encouraging substitution toward domestically produced alternatives. Option A describes a quota instead, option C describes an export subsidy, a different policy tool, and option D describes a trade agreement, the opposite of a protectionist tariff.

    Concept Tested: Tariff

    3. Why can two countries both gain from trade even if one country has an absolute advantage in producing every good?

    1. Because absolute advantage guarantees gains from trade on its own
    2. Because the less productive country can simply refuse to trade until prices equalize
    3. Because gains from trade depend on differing opportunity costs (comparative advantage), not on which country is more productive in an absolute sense
    4. Because trade always requires both countries to have exactly equal productivity
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. As long as the two countries' relative productivities (and therefore opportunity costs) differ, both can gain from specializing according to comparative advantage and trading, regardless of which country is absolutely more productive at everything. Option A misattributes the source of gains to absolute rather than comparative advantage, option B describes an unproductive strategy irrelevant to the actual mechanism, and option D is false since equal productivity would eliminate, not create, a basis for comparative-advantage-based trade.

    Concept Tested: Gains From Trade

    4. According to the Heckscher-Ohlin model, which good will a capital-abundant country tend to export?

    1. Labor-intensive goods, since capital-abundant countries always have surplus labor
    2. Whichever good happens to have the lowest world price
    3. Goods that use neither labor nor capital intensively
    4. Capital-intensive goods, since the country is relatively well-endowed with capital and has a comparative advantage in goods that use capital intensively
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The Heckscher-Ohlin theorem predicts a country exports the good that uses intensively the factor it holds in relative abundance, so a capital-abundant country like Germany tends to export capital-intensive goods such as precision machinery. Option A reverses the logic of factor abundance, option B introduces an unrelated pricing criterion, and option C describes an empty category not addressed by the model.

    Concept Tested: Heckscher-Ohlin Model

    5. Country A can produce 6 units of cloth or 3 units of wine per worker per day; Country B can produce 4 units of cloth or 4 units of wine per worker per day. Which country has a comparative advantage in cloth?

    1. Country A, since its opportunity cost of cloth (0.5 wine) is lower than Country B's (1.0 wine)
    2. Country B, since it has no absolute advantage in either good
    3. Neither country, since comparative advantage requires an absolute advantage in at least one good
    4. Country B, since it is equally productive in both goods
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Country A's opportunity cost of 1 unit of cloth is \(3/6=0.5\) units of wine, while Country B's is \(4/4=1.0\) unit of wine; since 0.5 is lower, Country A has the comparative advantage in cloth. Option B states a true fact about Country B but draws the wrong conclusion, and options C and D both incorrectly suggest that comparative advantage requires absolute advantage or equal productivity, neither of which is true.

    Concept Tested: Ricardian Trade Model

    6. A small country imports sugar at a world price of $0.30/lb, where domestic quantity demanded is 600 million lbs and domestic quantity supplied is 300 million lbs. The government imposes a $0.10/lb tariff, raising the domestic price to $0.40/lb, where quantity demanded falls to 540 million lbs and quantity supplied rises to 360 million lbs. What is the government's tariff revenue?

    1. $30 million
    2. $18 million
    3. $36 million
    4. $54 million
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Imports after the tariff equal \(540 - 360 = 180\) million lbs, so tariff revenue is \(\$0.10 \times 180\text{ million} = \$18\) million. Option A mistakenly uses the pre-tariff import quantity, and options C and D result from using an incorrect import figure or tariff rate in the calculation.

    Concept Tested: Tariff

    7. Country Z exports $420 billion worth of goods and services and imports $500 billion worth this year. What is Country Z's trade balance, and what must be true of its capital/financial account?

    1. Trade balance = +$80 billion (a surplus); capital/financial account must show an $80 billion deficit
    2. Trade balance = $920 billion; the capital/financial account must equal zero
    3. Trade balance = -$80 billion (a deficit); capital/financial account must show an $80 billion surplus (net capital inflow)
    4. Trade balance = -$80 billion (a deficit); the capital/financial account is unaffected since the two accounts are unrelated
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Exports minus imports is \(420-500=-\$80\) billion, a trade (current account) deficit; because the overall balance of payments must sum to zero, the capital/financial account must show an offsetting $80 billion surplus, meaning the country is a net recipient of foreign investment. Option A reverses the sign of the trade balance, option B incorrectly adds the two flows instead of subtracting them, and option D ignores the accounting identity that ties the two accounts together.

    Concept Tested: Trade Deficit

    8. A capital-abundant country opens to trade with a labor-abundant country. According to the Heckscher-Ohlin model and the analysis of winners and losers from trade, which group within the capital-abundant country is most likely to experience a relative decline in income?

    1. Capital owners, since the country will now export capital-intensive goods and import labor-intensive goods
    2. Consumers, since they will pay higher prices for all imported goods
    3. Export-oriented firms, since they will lose access to foreign markets
    4. Workers whose skills compete with imports of labor-intensive goods, since the country will import more labor-intensive goods, reducing domestic demand for labor in those industries
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. As the capital-abundant country shifts toward exporting capital-intensive goods and importing labor-intensive goods, workers whose jobs compete with those imports face reduced demand for their labor, making them the group most likely to lose in relative terms. Option A describes the opposite of the predicted effect (capital owners, the abundant factor's owners, tend to gain), option B is incorrect since trade generally lowers prices on imported goods rather than raising them, and option C misdescribes exporters, who typically gain from access to larger foreign markets rather than lose.

    Concept Tested: Trade Winners and Losers

    9. A government is deciding between imposing a tariff or an import quota to achieve the same reduction in imports of a good. Based on the chapter's comparison of the two policies, what is the key difference in who captures the price wedge created by each policy?

    1. Under a tariff, the price wedge is captured by the government as tariff revenue; under a quota, it becomes a quota rent typically captured by license holders or foreign exporters rather than the government
    2. There is no meaningful difference, since both policies always benefit the same group
    3. Under a quota, the government always captures more revenue than under an equivalent tariff
    4. Tariffs eliminate deadweight loss entirely, while quotas always create it
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. A tariff directly generates government revenue from the price wedge, while an equivalent quota creates the same price wedge as a quota rent, which typically accrues to whoever holds the import license or to foreign exporters, not the domestic government. Option B denies a real and important distinction, option C reverses the actual revenue relationship since quotas generally generate no direct government revenue at all, and option D incorrectly claims tariffs are free of deadweight loss, when the chapter explicitly shows they are not.

    Concept Tested: Quota Rent

    10. A policymaker argues that a new tariff on imported steel will "save American jobs" in the domestic steel industry. Evaluate this argument using the economic analysis of protectionism from this chapter.

    1. The argument is fully correct and requires no further analysis, since any tariff that protects an industry is unambiguously good policy
    2. The argument may be true narrowly (some steel-industry jobs may be preserved), but it ignores the broader costs: higher prices for all consumers and downstream industries that use steel, job losses in export industries facing retaliation, and empirical evidence that the cost per job "saved" often vastly exceeds the protected workers' wages
    3. The argument is entirely false, since tariffs never have any effect on domestic employment in the protected industry
    4. The argument cannot be evaluated at all, since economists have no tools for analyzing the effects of tariffs on employment
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. While a tariff can genuinely preserve some jobs in the protected industry, this narrow truth ignores the broader welfare costs the chapter documents: higher consumer prices, harm to downstream industries, potential job losses in retaliated-against export sectors, and empirical findings that the cost per job saved often reaches hundreds of thousands of dollars annually. Option A overstates the case by ignoring these documented costs entirely, option C overcorrects by denying any job-preserving effect at all, and option D wrongly claims the question is unanalyzable when the chapter provides exactly this kind of cost-benefit framework.

    Concept Tested: Protectionism
