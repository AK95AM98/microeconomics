    Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics

    Learning Objectives

    After completing this chapter, you will be able to:

    1. Define economics and distinguish microeconomics from macroeconomics
    2. Explain scarcity, choice, and the concept of opportunity cost
    3. Apply marginal analysis to decision-making
    4. Construct and interpret a Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)
    5. Identify the role of incentives and trade in economic reasoning

    1.1 What Is Economics?

    Economics is the study of how individuals, firms, and societies allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants. Because resources — time, money, land, labor — are limited, every economic agent must make choices. Economics provides the tools to analyze those choices systematically.

    Two Branches of Economics

    • Microeconomics studies individual decision-makers: households, firms, and specific markets. Why does coffee cost more than water? Why do nurses earn less than surgeons?
    • Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole: total output, unemployment, inflation, and economic growth. Why did GDP fall during COVID-19? What causes inflation?

    This textbook focuses on microeconomics.

    1.2 Scarcity and Choice

    The Fundamental Problem

    Scarcity means that resources are limited relative to human wants. This is the central fact of economic life. Because we cannot have everything, we must choose. Every choice involves a trade-off — getting more of one thing means getting less of another.

    Scarcity in everyday life

    You have 24 hours in a day. If you spend 3 hours studying economics, those 3 hours cannot also be spent sleeping, working, or watching TV. Time is scarce; you must allocate it.

    Opportunity Cost

    The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative you give up when you make that choice. It is the true cost of any decision.

    \[\text{Opportunity Cost} = \text{Value of the Next-Best Alternative Foregone}\]

    Opportunity cost of college

    The full cost of attending college is not just tuition and books. It also includes the foregone wages you could have earned by working instead. For a student who could earn $35,000/year, the opportunity cost of a four-year degree includes $140,000 in foregone income — even if tuition is free.

    Key insight: Opportunity costs are often hidden. Economists think about all costs — not just the ones that show up on a bill.

    Sunk Costs

    A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered. Rational decision-making requires ignoring sunk costs and focusing only on future costs and benefits.

    The sunk cost fallacy

    You bought a $15 movie ticket but the film is terrible. Should you stay to "get your money's worth"? No — the $15 is gone regardless. The only relevant question is: Will the next two hours of my life be better spent watching this movie or doing something else?

    1.3 Marginal Analysis

    Economists think at the margin. A marginal change is a small, incremental change. Marginal analysis compares the additional benefits of an action to its additional costs.

    The Rule: Pursue an activity as long as the marginal benefit (MB) exceeds or equals the marginal cost (MC).

    \[\text{Optimal choice: MB} = \text{MC}\]

    Example: How many hours to study?

    Suppose each additional hour of studying raises your exam grade, but with diminishing returns:

    Study Hours Marginal Benefit (grade points gained) Marginal Cost (value of time foregone)
    1st hour 20 5
    2nd hour 15 5
    3rd hour 10 5
    4th hour 5 5
    5th hour 2 5

    The optimal choice is 4 hours — where MB = MC = 5. Studying a 5th hour costs more than it's worth.

    Marginal thinking in business

    An airline has a flight from Minneapolis to Chicago that is half-full. Should it sell the remaining seats at a discount? Yes — as long as the discounted fare exceeds the marginal cost of adding a passenger (essentially zero for an already-flying plane). The fixed costs of the flight are sunk.

    Diminishing Marginal Returns

    As you do more of something, holding everything else constant, the marginal benefit typically falls. This is the principle of diminishing marginal returns (or diminishing marginal utility for consumers).

    • The first slice of pizza is wonderful. The fifth is less enjoyable.
    • The first hour of studying helps a lot. The tenth hour adds little.

    1.4 The Production Possibilities Frontier

    The Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF) is a model that illustrates the trade-offs facing an economy (or individual) that produces two goods using fixed resources.

    Constructing the PPF

    Consider a small economy that can produce only wheat and computers, using all available labor and capital.

    Production Possibility Wheat (millions of bushels) Computers (millions of units)
    A 0 30
    B 5 27
    C 10 21
    D 15 12
    E 18 0

    Plotting these combinations traces the PPF — a curve bowing outward from the axes.

    Key Features of the PPF

    1. Points on the curve — efficient: all resources are fully employed.

    2. Points inside the curve — inefficient: resources are idle or wasted (e.g., during a recession).

    3. Points outside the curve — currently unattainable with existing resources and technology.

    4. The slope of the PPF = the opportunity cost of producing one more unit of a good.

    Why Is the PPF Bowed Outward?

    The outward bow (concave shape) reflects the law of increasing opportunity costs: as you produce more of one good, you must give up increasing amounts of the other. This happens because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods — the most productive wheat farmers are moved to computer production last.

    \[\text{Opportunity Cost of Computers} = \frac{\Delta \text{Wheat}}{\Delta \text{Computers}}\]

    As we move from A to E (producing more wheat), the opportunity cost of each additional bushel of wheat rises.

    Shifts in the PPF

    The PPF shifts outward when:

    • More resources are available (e.g., population growth, capital investment)
    • Technology improves (higher productivity for one or both goods)

    Technological progress

    If a new variety of wheat doubles agricultural productivity, the PPF shifts outward along the wheat axis — the economy can now produce more wheat without sacrificing any computers.

    Comparative Advantage and Specialization

    Two individuals (or countries) can both gain by specializing in the good for which they have the lower opportunity cost and then trading. This is the principle of comparative advantage (covered in depth in Chapter 16).

    1.5 Economic Models and Assumptions

    Economists build models — simplified representations of reality — to isolate and study specific relationships. A model deliberately ignores some details to focus on what matters most.

    Common economic assumptions:

    • Ceteris paribus ("all else equal") — when analyzing one variable, hold others constant
    • Rational behavior — individuals make choices to maximize their well-being given constraints
    • Equilibrium — markets tend toward a state where supply equals demand

    Models are not perfect — they are useful

    A map of Minneapolis that showed every building, tree, and pothole would be unusable. A useful map simplifies. Economic models do the same thing: they sacrifice realism for insight.

    Positive vs. Normative Economics

    Positive Normative
    Nature Descriptive — what is Prescriptive — what ought to be
    Example "A minimum wage increase reduces employment for low-skill workers." "The minimum wage should be raised to $15/hour."
    Verifiable? In principle, yes No — depends on values

    Economists try to keep these separate. A positive analysis of the minimum wage does not tell you whether to support it; that depends on your values and how you weigh trade-offs.

    Key Terms

    Term Definition
    Scarcity The condition in which resources are limited relative to wants
    Opportunity Cost The value of the next-best alternative foregone
    Sunk Cost A past cost that cannot be recovered and should be ignored
    Marginal Analysis Decision-making by comparing marginal benefits to marginal costs
    Marginal Benefit (MB) The additional benefit from one more unit of an activity
    Marginal Cost (MC) The additional cost from one more unit of an activity
    Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF) A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce
    Efficiency Producing at a point on the PPF (no waste)
    Law of Increasing Opportunity Costs As more of a good is produced, its opportunity cost rises
    Comparative Advantage The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer
    Ceteris Paribus Latin for "all else equal" — a modeling assumption
    Positive Economics Analysis of what is (factual, testable)
    Normative Economics Analysis of what ought to be (value-based)

    Practice Problems

    Problem 1 — Opportunity Cost

    Maria is a dentist who earns $150/hour. She decides to spend a Saturday afternoon (4 hours) painting her living room rather than hiring a painter who charges $40/hour.

    a) What is the explicit cost of painting the room herself?
    b) What is the opportunity cost of painting the room herself?
    c) From an economic standpoint, is it rational for Maria to paint the room herself?

    Solution

    a) The explicit cost is $0 — she is not paying anyone.

    b) The opportunity cost is the foregone dentist income: 4 hours × $150/hour = $600.

    c) The total economic cost of Maria painting is $600 (opportunity cost) compared to $160 (paying the painter). It is not rational from a purely economic standpoint — she should hire the painter and spend those 4 hours working as a dentist.

    Problem 2 — Sunk Cost

    You paid $80 for a non-refundable ticket to a concert. On the day of the concert, you feel slightly ill and estimate that attending will give you $30 of enjoyment, while staying home and resting will give you $60 of enjoyment.

    a) Should you attend the concert? Why or why not?
    b) Does the $80 ticket price affect your decision?

    Solution

    a) You should stay home. The relevant comparison is: $30 (attending) vs. $60 (resting). Resting gives you more utility.

    b) No. The $80 is a sunk cost — it is gone regardless of what you do. Rational decision-making ignores sunk costs.

    Problem 3 — Marginal Analysis

    A firm's data on weekly output is shown below:

    Workers Total Output (units) Marginal Product Marginal Revenue ($10/unit) Marginal Labor Cost ($200/worker)
    1 50 50 $500 $200
    2 95 45 $450 $200
    3 130 35 $350 $200
    4 155 25 $250 $200
    5 170 15 $150 $200
    6 180 10 $100 $200

    a) How many workers should the firm hire?
    b) What principle guides this decision?

    Solution

    a) The firm should hire 4 workers. At 4 workers, the marginal revenue product ($250) still exceeds the marginal cost ($200). Adding a 5th worker costs $200 but generates only $150 in revenue — a net loss.

    b) The MB = MC principle: hire as long as the marginal benefit (marginal revenue product) exceeds the marginal cost (wage).

    Problem 4 — Production Possibilities Frontier

    An economy produces only food and clothing. With all resources devoted to food, it can produce 120 units. With all resources devoted to clothing, it can produce 60 units. Assume the PPF is a straight line.

    a) Draw the PPF and label the intercepts.
    b) What is the opportunity cost of 1 unit of clothing in terms of food?
    c) What is the opportunity cost of 1 unit of food in terms of clothing?
    d) If the economy is producing at (60 food, 30 clothing), is it efficient?

    Solution

    a) The PPF runs from (0 food, 60 clothing) to (120 food, 0 clothing) — a straight line.

    b) Opportunity cost of 1 unit of clothing = 120/60 = 2 units of food.

    c) Opportunity cost of 1 unit of food = 60/120 = 0.5 units of clothing.

    d) The point (60, 30) lies exactly on the PPF (it satisfies the constraint: 60/120 + 30/60 = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1). This is an efficient point.

    Problem 5 — Positive vs. Normative

    Classify each statement as positive or normative.

    1. "The United States spends about 18% of GDP on healthcare."
    2. "The government should provide universal healthcare."
    3. "Rent control reduces the quantity of housing supplied."
    4. "Housing is a human right and should not be subject to market prices."
    Solution
    1. Positive — a factual, measurable claim.
    2. Normative — a value judgment about policy.
    3. Positive — a testable prediction of economic theory.
    4. Normative — a statement about values, not facts.
