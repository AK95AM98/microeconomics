    Chapter 8: Monopolistic Competition

    Learning Objectives

    After completing this chapter, you will be able to:

    1. List the characteristics that define monopolistic competition
    2. Explain how product differentiation and brand loyalty give firms some pricing power
    3. Find a monopolistically competitive firm's short-run profit-maximizing price and quantity
    4. Explain why economic profit is driven to zero in the long run and show this graphically
    5. Explain excess capacity and why monopolistically competitive firms price above marginal cost
    6. Analyze the economic role of advertising and other non-price competition
    7. Weigh the efficiency costs of monopolistic competition against the benefits of product variety

    The Blend of Both Worlds

    This chapter sits right between the last two: many firms like perfect competition, but each with a sliver of pricing power like a monopolist. Think about your own favorite coffee shop as you read — every choice has a trade-off, and here the trade-off is between efficiency and the variety you actually enjoy.

    8.1 Characteristics of Monopolistic Competition

    Monopolistic competition describes a market structure that combines features of both perfect competition and monopoly:

    Feature Perfect Competition Monopolistic Competition Monopoly
    Number of firms Many Many One
    Product Identical Differentiated Unique (no substitutes)
    Entry/exit Free Free Blocked
    Price-setting power None (price taker) Some Full (price maker)
    Long-run economic profit Zero Zero Can be positive

    Think of restaurants, hair salons, clothing brands, coffee shops, or gas stations in a city: there are many firms, entry is relatively easy, but each firm's product is slightly different from its rivals' — a Chipotle burrito is not identical to a Qdoba burrito, even though both compete in the "fast casual Mexican food" market.

    Monopolistically competitive industries

    • Restaurants and coffee shops
    • Clothing and shoe brands
    • Hair salons and barbershops
    • Smartphone apps in a given category (e.g., note-taking apps)

    8.2 Product Differentiation and Brand Loyalty

    Product differentiation means firms make their product distinct from competitors' in the eyes of consumers — through physical features, location, service quality, branding, or perceived quality.

    Differentiation matters because it gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve (unlike the horizontal demand curve of a perfectly competitive firm). If a monopolistically competitive firm raises its price slightly, it loses some customers to rivals, but not all of them — because its product isn't a perfect substitute for theirs.

    Brand loyalty is the tendency of some consumers to keep buying a firm's product even when a rival offers a lower price or a seemingly identical product. Loyalty is built through advertising, consistent quality, and habit, and it is what allows firms to charge a price above marginal cost without losing every customer.

    Why the firm's demand curve is downward-sloping but highly elastic

    Because many competitors offer close (though not perfect) substitutes, a monopolistically competitive firm's demand curve, while downward-sloping, is typically much flatter (more elastic) than a true monopolist's. Raising price loses a lot of customers to rivals — just not literally all of them, as it would for a perfectly competitive firm.

    A Flatter Monopoly, Not a Weaker Competitor

    It helps to think of each monopolistically competitive firm as a tiny monopolist over its own small slice of brand loyalty — just one facing unusually flat, elastic demand because so many close substitutes exist nearby.

    8.3 Short-Run Profit Maximization

    In the short run, a monopolistically competitive firm behaves exactly like a monopolist: it faces a downward-sloping demand curve \(D\) (and corresponding marginal revenue curve \(MR\), which lies below \(D\)), and it maximizes profit where:

    \[MR = MC\]

    It then charges the price given by the demand curve at that quantity.

    Worked Example

    A local coffee shop faces demand \(P = 8 - 0.02Q\) (price in dollars, \(Q\) in cups per week) and has marginal cost \(MC = 2\) with fixed cost \(FC = 400\) per week (so \(TC = 400 + 2Q\)).

    Marginal revenue: \(TR = PQ = 8Q - 0.02Q^2 \Rightarrow MR = 8 - 0.04Q\).

    Set \(MR = MC\):

    \[8 - 0.04Q = 2\]
    \[6 = 0.04Q\]
    \[Q = 150\]

    Price: \(P = 8 - 0.02(150) = 8 - 3 = \$5\).

    \(ATC\) at \(Q=150\): \(ATC = \dfrac{400 + 2(150)}{150} = \dfrac{700}{150} \approx \$4.67\).

    Since \(P = \$5.00 > ATC \approx \$4.67\), the shop earns a short-run economic profit of approximately \((5.00 - 4.67) \times 150 \approx \$50\) per week.

    Value
    Profit-maximizing quantity 150 cups/week
    Price $5.00
    \(ATC\) $4.67
    Economic profit ≈ $50/week

    8.4 Long-Run Equilibrium: Zero Economic Profit

    Short-run profits like the one above are a signal. Because entry is free in monopolistic competition, the coffee shop's success attracts new competitors — new coffee shops, cafés, and even non-coffee alternatives that split the market.

    The Entry Mechanism

    1. Existing firms earn positive economic profit in the short run.
    2. New firms enter, offering their own differentiated versions of the product.
    3. Entry shifts each incumbent firm's demand curve to the left (and makes it more elastic/flatter) — the same total market demand is now shared among more sellers, and each firm's product has more close substitutes than before.
    4. Entry continues until demand has shifted left enough that price equals average total cost everywhere along the firm's residual demand curve at its profit-maximizing quantity — economic profit falls to zero.
    5. If firms were suffering losses, the reverse happens: exit shifts remaining firms' demand curves right until losses disappear.

    The Long-Run Equilibrium Condition

    In long-run equilibrium, two conditions hold simultaneously:

    \[MR = MC \quad \text{(profit-maximizing quantity)}\]
    \[P = ATC \quad \text{(zero economic profit)}\]

    Graphically, this means the firm's downward-sloping demand curve is tangent to its \(ATC\) curve at the profit-maximizing quantity — the demand curve touches \(ATC\) at exactly one point, from above, without crossing it. If demand sat above \(ATC\) at the best quantity, profit would still be positive and more entry would occur; if it sat entirely below, firms would be exiting.

    Tangency, step by step

    Starting from the coffee shop's short-run profit in Section 8.3, new cafés enter the neighborhood. Demand for our shop's coffee shrinks and becomes more elastic. This process continues — shifting demand left — until the best price the shop can charge exactly covers its average total cost. At that point, there is no longer an incentive for further entry.

    Watch the Demand Curve Walk Left

    Three snapshots of a shifting demand curve can feel like a lot to track at once. Focus on just one thing per row: as demand shifts left, find the new \(MR=MC\) quantity, then check whether \(P\) still beats \(ATC\).

    Tracking the Coffee Shop Through Entry

    Continuing the example from Section 8.3, suppose entry gradually shifts the coffee shop's demand curve inward. The table below illustrates three snapshots of this adjustment process (illustrative, not derived from a single equation) as new shops open nearby:

    Stage Demand Curve \(Q^*\) (where \(MR=MC\)) \(P^*\) \(ATC\) at \(Q^*\) Economic Profit
    Short run (Section 8.3) \(P = 8 - 0.02Q\) 150 $5.00 $4.67 ≈ +$50/week
    After some entry \(P = 7 - 0.02Q\) 125 $4.50 $4.40 ≈ +$12.50/week
    Long-run equilibrium \(P = 6.4 - 0.02Q\) 110 $4.20 $4.20 $0/week

    As rival cafés open, the demand curve shifts down and to the left, the profit-maximizing quantity falls, and profit shrinks toward zero. Entry stops exactly when price just equals \(ATC\) at the firm's best quantity.

    8.5 Excess Capacity and the Markup

    Even though long-run economic profit is zero, monopolistic competition does not replicate the perfectly competitive outcome. Two differences remain:

    Price Above Marginal Cost (the Markup)

    Because the firm's demand curve is downward-sloping (not horizontal), the tangency condition \(P = ATC\) occurs where \(MR = MC\), and since \(MR < P\) for a downward-sloping demand curve, it must be that:

    \[P > MC\]

    even in long-run equilibrium. The firm retains a small markup over marginal cost, unlike a perfectly competitive firm, which drives \(P = MC\).

    Spot the Markup Fast

    Any time a firm's demand curve slopes downward, \(MR\) sits below it — so setting \(MR = MC\) automatically leaves \(P > MC\). You don't need to recompute this each time; it's baked into any downward-sloping demand curve.

    Excess Capacity

    The tangency between demand and \(ATC\) must occur at a quantity to the left of the minimum point of the \(ATC\) curve (where \(ATC\) is still declining). This is because a downward-sloping demand curve can only be tangent to a U-shaped \(ATC\) curve on its declining portion.

    This means each firm in long-run equilibrium produces less than the output that would minimize its average total cost. The gap between the firm's actual output and the output at minimum \(ATC\) is called excess capacity:

    \[\text{Excess Capacity} = Q_{\min ATC} - Q^*\]

    Interpreting excess capacity

    If the coffee shop merely served more cups at the same quality, its average cost per cup would fall. It doesn't, because demand for any single shop isn't large enough at a profitable price. This is why monopolistically competitive markets tend to have "too many" firms, each operating below their efficient scale — think of how many lightly-patronized coffee shops and nail salons can coexist on a few blocks of a city.

    Perfect Competition (long run) Monopolistic Competition (long run)
    Economic profit Zero Zero
    \(P\) vs. \(MC\) \(P = MC\) \(P > MC\)
    Output relative to minimum \(ATC\) At minimum \(ATC\) (efficient scale) Below minimum \(ATC\) (excess capacity)
    Product variety None (identical goods) Significant

    Zero Profit Doesn't Mean 'Just Like Competition'

    Long-run economic profit hits zero in both market structures, but don't stop there — monopolistic competition still leaves \(P > MC\) and excess capacity behind, two inefficiencies a truly competitive market never has.

    8.6 Advertising and Non-Price Competition

    Because price competition alone cannot fully differentiate a monopolistically competitive firm's product, firms frequently rely on non-price competition — advertising, packaging, location, customer service, and other means of making their product seem different (or better) without changing its price.

    Economic Effects of Advertising

    Advertising has two competing economic interpretations:

    View Argument
    Informative view Advertising provides useful information about product existence, price, and quality, helping consumers make better choices and lowering their search costs
    Persuasive view Advertising manipulates preferences, creates artificial brand loyalty, and raises barriers to entry (new firms must spend heavily to build a comparable brand), without adding real value

    Both effects are present in practice. Advertising can raise a firm's fixed costs (another reason output may remain below the cost-minimizing scale) but can also expand the firm's customer base enough to lower average costs by spreading large fixed costs over more sales — the net effect on prices is ambiguous and depends on the specific market.

    Advertising and generic drugs

    Advertising intensity correlates with brand loyalty: heavily advertised brand-name drugs often sell at a large premium over chemically identical generics, even after the patent expires and legal barriers to entry disappear. This illustrates the persuasive view — advertising alone sustains a price gap unrelated to any real product difference.

    8.7 Welfare Analysis: Efficiency vs. Variety

    Monopolistic competition is not allocatively efficient in the strict sense used for perfect competition: price exceeds marginal cost, and firms operate with excess capacity rather than at their efficient scale. In isolation, this looks like a cost to society, similar to (though usually smaller than) the deadweight loss created by a monopolist.

    However, a purely efficiency-focused comparison misses something perfectly competitive markets cannot provide: product variety. Consumers place real value on having many distinct options — different cuisines, different store locations, different brands with different features — rather than one standardized product sold at minimum cost.

    The Trade-off

    Benefit Cost
    Fewer, larger, more standardized firms Lower average cost per unit (closer to efficient scale) Less product variety; less convenient locations; fewer choices
    Many smaller, differentiated firms Greater product variety and convenience Higher average cost per unit; price above marginal cost; some deadweight loss

    Whether monopolistic competition is "good" or "bad" for society, therefore, depends on how much consumers value variety relative to the efficiency loss from excess capacity and markups. Most economists view monopolistic competition as a reasonably efficient compromise: it delivers substantial variety at a modest efficiency cost, which is why it describes so much of the real-world retail and service economy.

    A note on 'wasteful' competition

    It is tempting to call duplicated storefronts, overlapping product lines, and advertising "wasteful." But in a world where consumers demonstrably value choice — different flavors, different neighborhoods, different aesthetics — some of this apparent waste is actually consumers paying (via slightly higher prices) for variety they want.

    Key Terms

    Term Definition
    Monopolistic Competition A market structure with many firms, free entry and exit, and differentiated products
    Product Differentiation Making a firm's product distinct from competitors' in consumers' eyes
    Brand Loyalty Consumers' tendency to keep buying a firm's product despite lower-priced alternatives
    Short-Run Equilibrium The price and quantity where \(MR = MC\) for a monopolistically competitive firm
    Long-Run Equilibrium The zero-economic-profit outcome where the demand curve is tangent to \(ATC\)
    Markup The amount by which price exceeds marginal cost
    Excess Capacity The gap between a firm's actual output and the output that minimizes average total cost
    Non-Price Competition Competing through advertising, quality, service, or location rather than price
    Informative Advertising Advertising that reduces consumer search costs by conveying real product information
    Persuasive Advertising Advertising that shapes preferences and builds brand loyalty unrelated to real product differences
    Product Variety The range of differentiated options available to consumers in a market
    Tangency Condition The long-run condition where the firm's demand curve just touches its \(ATC\) curve

    Practice Problems

    Problem 1 — Characteristics

    For each market, identify whether it is best described as perfect competition, monopolistic competition, or monopoly, and justify your answer briefly.

    a) Wheat farming
    b) Nail salons in a large city
    c) The local electric utility
    d) Smartphone photo-editing apps

    Solution

    a) Perfect competition — identical product (wheat is a commodity), many sellers, price takers.

    b) Monopolistic competition — many salons, free entry, but each differentiated by location, staff, ambiance, and service quality.

    c) Monopoly — a single firm supplies the entire local market; high fixed-cost barriers to entry (natural monopoly).

    d) Monopolistic competition — many apps compete, entry is relatively easy (low cost to publish an app), but each app differentiates through features, design, and branding.

    Problem 2 — Short-Run Profit Maximization

    A boutique clothing store faces demand \(P = 60 - 0.5Q\) and has \(MC = 10\) with fixed costs of \(FC = 200\) (so \(TC = 200 + 10Q\)).

    a) Find the profit-maximizing price and quantity.
    b) Calculate \(ATC\) at that quantity.
    c) Is the store earning a short-run economic profit, a loss, or breaking even? By how much?

    Solution

    a) \(TR = 60Q - 0.5Q^2 \Rightarrow MR = 60 - Q\). Set \(MR = MC\): \(60 - Q = 10 \Rightarrow Q = 50\). \(P = 60 - 0.5(50) = \$35\).

    b) \(ATC = \dfrac{200 + 10(50)}{50} = \dfrac{700}{50} = \$14\).

    c) \(P = \$35 > ATC = \$14\), so the store earns a short-run economic profit of \((35-14) \times 50 = \$1{,}050\).

    Problem 3 — Long-Run Adjustment

    Using the boutique from Problem 2, suppose the $1,050 short-run profit attracts new clothing boutiques into the neighborhood.

    a) What happens to the original boutique's demand curve as new competitors enter?
    b) What happens to its profit over time?
    c) What condition characterizes the new long-run equilibrium?

    Solution

    a) The demand curve shifts left (fewer customers per firm at any price) and becomes more elastic (flatter), since more close substitutes are now available nearby.

    b) Profit shrinks toward zero as entry continues, because the demand curve keeps shifting left (and becoming more elastic) until no further profit is available to attract entry.

    c) Long-run equilibrium occurs where \(MR = MC\) and \(P = ATC\) simultaneously — the (now-shifted) demand curve is tangent to the \(ATC\) curve at the firm's profit-maximizing quantity.

    Problem 4 — Excess Capacity

    In long-run equilibrium, a bakery's \(ATC\) curve reaches its minimum value of $3.00 per loaf at \(Q = 300\) loaves per week. In its actual long-run equilibrium, the bakery produces 220 loaves per week at a price of $3.40, which equals its \(ATC\) at that quantity.

    a) What is the bakery's excess capacity?
    b) Is price above or below marginal cost at the equilibrium quantity? Explain why this must be true given the tangency condition.
    c) Does this bakery earn positive, zero, or negative economic profit in the long run? Explain.

    Solution

    a) Excess capacity \(= Q_{\min ATC} - Q^* = 300 - 220 = 80\) loaves per week.

    b) Price is above marginal cost. Because the firm's demand curve slopes downward, \(MR < P\) at every quantity. Since the firm sets \(MR = MC\), and \(MR < P\), it follows that \(MC < P\) — the markup is a direct consequence of facing a downward-sloping (not horizontal) demand curve.

    c) Economic profit is zero, because price equals \(ATC\) ($3.40 = $3.40) exactly at the equilibrium quantity — this is the defining feature of long-run equilibrium under monopolistic competition.

    Problem 5 — Advertising and Welfare

    A regional airline begins a major advertising campaign emphasizing its loyalty rewards program, successfully increasing brand loyalty among business travelers.

    a) How would you expect this to affect the airline's demand curve (shape and position)?
    b) How would you expect it to affect the airline's markup over marginal cost?
    c) Does this advertising represent the "informative" or "persuasive" view, and does it necessarily reduce consumer welfare? Explain both sides.

    Solution

    a) The demand curve should shift right (more customers at every price) and become less elastic / steeper (more loyal customers are less willing to switch to a rival even if price rises).

    b) A less elastic demand curve allows a larger markup over marginal cost — the airline can raise price further above \(MC\) without losing as many customers.

    c) This is closer to the persuasive view, since loyalty programs are explicitly designed to make switching costly rather than to convey new information about the product itself. However, it is not purely wasteful: loyalty programs can also provide real value (discounts, perks, convenience) that consumers genuinely appreciate, so some of the brand loyalty built reflects real, not manufactured, value — the welfare effect is ambiguous and depends on how much of the loyalty reflects real benefits versus pure habit or switching costs.

    You Can Explain Your Own Neighborhood

    You can now explain why there are five coffee shops within walking distance, none of them huge, all charging slightly different prices — and why that's a real trade-off between efficiency and the variety you get to choose from!
