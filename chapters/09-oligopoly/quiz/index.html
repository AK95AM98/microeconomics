    Quiz: Oligopoly

    Test your understanding of the Cournot and Bertrand models, collusion, the kinked demand curve, and market concentration measures with these review questions.

    1. What defines an "oligopoly"?

    1. A market with a small number of large sellers whose decisions affect, and are affected by, each other
    2. A market with many small sellers producing an identical product
    3. A market with a single seller facing no close substitutes
    4. A market with many firms selling differentiated products and free entry
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Oligopoly is defined by a small number of large sellers who are strategically interdependent — each must anticipate rivals' reactions to its own pricing and output decisions. Option B describes perfect competition, option C describes monopoly, and option D describes monopolistic competition.

    Concept Tested: Oligopoly

    2. What does "strategic interdependence" mean in the context of oligopoly?

    1. Firms are too small individually to affect the market price
    2. A single firm can set price without regard to any competitor
    3. Each firm's optimal decision depends on the other firms' decisions, requiring each to anticipate rivals' choices
    4. Firms are legally required to coordinate their pricing decisions
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Strategic interdependence means an oligopolist cannot maximize profit by looking only at its own costs and demand; it must also anticipate how rivals will respond to its choices, since their decisions are affected by and affect its own. Option A describes a perfectly competitive firm, option B describes a monopolist with no rivals, and option D incorrectly implies a legal mandate rather than a strategic necessity.

    Concept Tested: Strategic Interdependence

    3. What is the "Bertrand paradox"?

    1. The finding that cartels always collapse immediately after formation
    2. The observation that prices in oligopoly rarely change despite cost fluctuations
    3. The finding that quantity competition always results in higher prices than price competition
    4. The result that even with only two identical firms, price competition drives price all the way down to marginal cost, matching the perfectly competitive outcome
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. The Bertrand paradox shows that with identical products and no capacity constraints, just two firms undercutting each other on price will compete price all the way down to marginal cost, the same zero-economic-profit outcome as perfect competition. Option A describes a separate point about cartel instability, option B describes the kinked demand curve model instead, and option C is factually backwards, since Bertrand (price) competition produces lower prices than Cournot (quantity) competition.

    Concept Tested: Bertrand Paradox

    4. Why do Cournot (quantity-setting) and Bertrand (price-setting) models predict different market outcomes for the same two firms, same costs, and same demand?

    1. Because Cournot firms always collude while Bertrand firms never do
    2. Because the strategic variable firms compete on — quantity or price — changes the nature of the competitive pressure each firm faces, even holding the number of firms and costs fixed
    3. Because Bertrand firms have higher marginal costs than Cournot firms by assumption
    4. Because Cournot competition only applies to monopolies
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Holding the number of firms, costs, and demand fixed, choosing to compete on quantity (Cournot) versus price (Bertrand) changes the equilibrium outcome entirely — Cournot firms land on a price above marginal cost, while Bertrand firms are driven to price at marginal cost. Option A invents an unsupported collusion assumption, option C introduces a cost difference the models do not assume, and option D is false since Cournot describes competition among multiple firms, not monopoly.

    Concept Tested: Cournot Model

    5. Why is collusion between oligopolists inherently unstable, even though it raises joint profit above the competitive level?

    1. Each member faces an incentive to secretly produce more than its quota, since it can earn extra profit by cheating as long as rivals don't retaliate — a dynamic captured by the prisoner's dilemma
    2. Because antitrust law makes all collusion physically impossible to carry out
    3. Because marginal cost rises so much under collusion that firms cannot profit from it
    4. Because collusion always involves more than two firms, making coordination impossible by definition
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Even though restricting joint output raises total cartel profit, each individual member can earn extra profit by secretly producing beyond its quota while price still sits near the monopoly level, and because every member faces this same temptation, the agreement tends to unravel — exactly the logic of a prisoner's dilemma. Option B overstates antitrust enforcement, which deters but does not physically prevent collusion (as OPEC among sovereign nations illustrates), option C invents an unsupported cost claim, and option D is false since collusion is entirely possible, and modeled, between just two firms.

    Concept Tested: Collusion

    6. According to the kinked demand curve model, why might an oligopolist leave its price unchanged even after a moderate rise in its marginal cost?

    1. Because demand is perfectly inelastic at every price for this firm
    2. Because marginal revenue is constant regardless of output
    3. Because rivals are assumed to match any price cut but ignore any price increase, making both price changes unattractive relative to holding price steady
    4. Because the firm has no marginal cost curve in oligopoly
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The kinked demand curve model assumes rivals ignore a price increase (so demand is highly elastic above the current price) but match a price cut (so demand is much less elastic below it), making both a price rise and a price cut unattractive, which keeps a range of marginal costs all pointing to the same unchanged price. Option A misstates the shape of demand, option B contradicts the very discontinuity in marginal revenue that drives the result, and option D is false since every firm, including an oligopolist, has a marginal cost curve.

    Concept Tested: Kinked Demand Curve

    7. Two firms compete in a Cournot duopoly with demand \(P = 140 - (Q_1+Q_2)\) and identical marginal cost \(c=20\). What is each firm's equilibrium quantity?

    1. 30
    2. 50
    3. 60
    4. 40
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Each firm's best response is \(Q_i = (120 - Q_j)/2\); imposing symmetry, \(Q^* = (120-Q^*)/2 \implies 3Q^* = 120 \implies Q^* = 40\). The other options come from arithmetic slips in solving the best-response equation or from confusing this quantity with total industry output or the monopoly quantity.

    Concept Tested: Cournot Model

    8. An industry has three firms with market shares of 50%, 30%, and 20%. What is the industry's Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)?

    1. 2,800
    2. 3,800
    3. 4,200
    4. 3,000
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. \(HHI = 50^2 + 30^2 + 20^2 = 2{,}500 + 900 + 400 = 3{,}800\). The other options result from squaring only some of the shares correctly or from arithmetic slips in adding the squared terms.

    Concept Tested: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index

    9. An industry has five firms with market shares of 35%, 25%, 20%, 10%, and 10%. What is the four-firm concentration ratio (CR4)?

    1. 70%
    2. 80%
    3. 90%
    4. 100%
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. \(CR_4\) sums the four largest shares: \(35+25+20+10 = 90\%\), excluding only the smallest firm's 10% share. Option A and B undercount by excluding more than one firm, and option D incorrectly includes all five firms' shares rather than just the top four.

    Concept Tested: Concentration Ratio

    10. Suppose an industry has an HHI of 2,800 (highly concentrated) before a proposed merger, and the merger would raise HHI to 3,300 (an increase of 500 points). Based on DOJ/FTC merger guidelines, how would regulators most likely respond?

    1. Approve the merger automatically, since any HHI above 2,500 is already considered efficient
    2. Subject the merger to significant scrutiny, since the market is already highly concentrated (HHI > 2,500) and the merger would increase HHI well beyond the 100-point threshold that raises concern
    3. Ignore the HHI figures entirely, since merger review relies solely on the concentration ratio, not HHI
    4. Give the merger minimal scrutiny, since the increase of 500 points is too small to matter in a highly concentrated market
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Because the market is already highly concentrated (HHI above 2,500) and the merger would increase HHI by 500 points, far beyond the 100-point threshold that raises concern under DOJ/FTC guidelines, this merger would face significant antitrust scrutiny. Option A misreads a high HHI as a sign of efficiency rather than a concentration concern, option C ignores that regulators do rely heavily on HHI, and option D understates how large a 500-point increase is relative to the guidelines' threshold.

    Concept Tested: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index
