# Glossary of Terms

#### Absolute Advantage

The ability to produce more output of a good than another producer using the same amount of resources.

**Example:** A farmer who can grow 100 bushels of corn per acre has an absolute advantage over a neighbor who grows only 60 bushels per acre on identical land.

#### Accounting Profit

The difference between a firm's total revenue and its explicit costs, such as wages, rent, and materials, not including the implicit opportunity cost of owner-supplied resources.

#### Adverse Selection

A problem arising from information asymmetry in which the party with less information ends up disproportionately attracting the riskiest or lowest-quality counterparts before a transaction occurs.

**Example:** An insurance company offering the same health insurance premium to everyone may attract mostly sicker applicants, since healthier people find the premium less worthwhile, a case of adverse selection.

#### Adverse Selection Death Spiral

A process in which rising prices caused by adverse selection drive out the lowest-risk participants, which raises average risk further and prices even more, potentially unraveling the market entirely.

**Example:** In a health insurance market, rising premiums driven by sicker enrollees can push healthier people to drop coverage altogether, triggering an adverse selection death spiral that keeps pushing premiums higher.

#### Agency Cost

The cost that arises from the conflict of interest and imperfect monitoring in a principal-agent relationship, including the cost of incentives offered and any residual loss from the agent not acting entirely in the principal's interest.

**Example:** The expense of auditing a company's managers, plus any profit lost because those managers still pursue some of their own goals, together make up the agency cost.

#### Allocative Efficiency

A condition in which resources are allocated so that producing one more unit of a good provides a benefit to society exactly equal to its cost, meaning price equals marginal cost.

**Example:** Perfectly competitive markets in long-run equilibrium achieve allocative efficiency because price settles where it equals marginal cost.

#### Antitrust Policy

Government laws and enforcement actions aimed at preventing monopoly power, breaking up excessive market concentration, and prohibiting anti-competitive business practices.

**Example:** A government blocking a merger between two large competing companies because it would substantially reduce competition is an application of antitrust policy.

#### Arbitrage

The practice of buying a good at a low price in one market and reselling it at a higher price in another, which tends to undermine price discrimination by narrowing price gaps.

**Example:** If a wholesaler buys a discounted student-priced product and resells it to non-students at a markup, that arbitrage can erode the seller's ability to price discriminate.

#### Average Fixed Cost

Fixed cost divided by the quantity of output produced, which falls continuously as output increases.

#### Average Product

The total output produced divided by the quantity of a variable input used, measuring output per unit of that input.

#### Average Total Cost

Total cost divided by the quantity of output produced, equal to the sum of average fixed cost and average variable cost.

**Example:** With a fixed cost of $1,000 and a variable cost of $500 for 100 units, average total cost is $15 per unit.

#### Average Variable Cost

Variable cost divided by the quantity of output produced; it shows the variable cost per unit at a given production level.

**Example:** If total variable cost is $500 at an output of 100 units, average variable cost is $5 per unit.

#### Average-Cost Pricing Regulation

A regulatory approach that requires a monopoly to set its price equal to its average total cost, allowing the firm to break even without earning excess profit, though output remains below the fully efficient level.

#### Backward Induction

A method for solving a sequential game by analyzing the last decisions first and working backward to determine what each player should rationally do at each earlier stage.

**Example:** Using backward induction, an entrant predicts how the incumbent would respond to entry, and makes its entry decision based on that predicted response.

#### Backward-Bending Labor Supply

A pattern in which a worker's labor supply curve slopes upward at low wages but bends backward at high wages, because the income effect eventually outweighs the substitution effect.

**Example:** A backward-bending labor supply curve could show a worker increasing hours as wages rise from $15 to $30 per hour, but then reducing hours as the wage rises further to $60.

#### Balance of Payments

A comprehensive record of all economic transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world during a given period, including trade, income flows, and financial investments.

**Example:** A country's balance of payments tracks not just exports and imports of goods, but also tourist spending, dividends paid to foreign investors, and cross-border purchases of stocks and bonds.

#### Barrier to Entry

An obstacle that prevents or discourages new firms from entering a market and competing with existing firms.

**Example:** A patent on a new drug is a barrier to entry because it legally prevents other firms from producing the same medicine for a set period.

#### Bertrand Model

A model of oligopoly in which firms simultaneously choose the prices they will charge for an identical product, and consumers buy from the lowest-price seller.

**Example:** In the Bertrand model, if one firm prices even slightly below another, it captures the entire market, driving fierce price competition.

#### Bertrand Paradox

The result of the Bertrand model that, even with only two firms producing an identical good, competitive price-cutting drives price down to marginal cost, the same outcome as under perfect competition.

**Example:** The Bertrand paradox shows that having just two competing sellers of an identical product can be enough to eliminate all markup above cost.

#### Bounded Rationality

The idea that people make decisions with limited information, limited time, and limited cognitive ability, rather than through the perfectly rational, unlimited calculation assumed in simple economic models.

**Example:** A shopper who picks a "good enough" cereal brand after a quick glance at the shelf, rather than calculating the best possible option among hundreds, is exhibiting bounded rationality.

#### Brand Loyalty

A buyer's tendency to continue purchasing a particular firm's product even when similar, and sometimes cheaper, alternatives are available.

#### Budget Constraint

The limit on the combinations of goods a consumer can purchase, determined by income and the prices of available goods.

**Example:** With $20 of income and apples priced at $2 each, a consumer's budget constraint limits her to at most 10 apples if she buys nothing else.

#### Cap-and-Trade

A market-based pollution control system in which the government sets a total limit on emissions and issues tradable permits that firms can buy and sell, letting the market determine the cheapest way to meet the cap.

**Example:** Under cap-and-trade, a firm that can cut its emissions cheaply might sell its unused permits to a firm facing higher cleanup costs, reducing overall pollution at lower total cost.

#### Cartel

A formal agreement among independent firms in the same industry to coordinate pricing and output in order to act collectively like a monopoly.

**Example:** An international group of oil-producing countries that meets to set production quotas and influence the world price of oil functions as a cartel.

#### Ceteris Paribus

A Latin phrase meaning "other things being equal," used to isolate the effect of one variable by holding all other relevant variables constant.

#### Change in Demand

A shift of the entire demand curve to a new position, caused by a change in a determinant of demand other than the good's own price.

#### Change in Supply

A shift of the entire supply curve to a new position, caused by a change in a determinant of supply other than the good's own price.

#### Choice

A decision to use a limited resource, such as time or money, for one purpose instead of another available purpose.

#### Coase Theorem

The proposition that, when transaction costs are low and property rights are clearly defined, private parties can negotiate their own efficient solution to an externality problem without government intervention.

**Example:** The Coase theorem suggests that a factory and a nearby laundry harmed by its smoke could reach a private deal, such as the factory paying for better filters, that resolves the externality efficiently on their own.

#### Cognitive Bias

A systematic, predictable pattern of deviation from fully rational judgment or decision-making, often arising from mental shortcuts rather than deliberate error.

**Example:** Overestimating the chance of a plane crash after seeing news coverage of one is a cognitive bias, since the actual risk has not changed.

#### Collusion

A secret or informal agreement among competing firms to coordinate prices, output, or other decisions in order to increase their joint profits.

**Example:** Rival gas stations quietly agreeing to all raise prices on the same day, without a formal contract, is an example of collusion.

#### Common-Pool Resource

A good that is rival in consumption but non-excludable, meaning it is difficult to prevent access but one person's use reduces what remains for others.

**Example:** Fish in an open ocean are a common-pool resource, since anyone can fish there but each fish caught is no longer available to other fishers.

#### Comparative Advantage

The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer, regardless of who is more skilled overall.

**Example:** If a lawyer can also type faster than her assistant but earns far more per hour practicing law, she has a comparative advantage in law, not typing, so she should focus on legal work and leave typing to the assistant.

#### Complement Goods

Two goods for which an increase in the price of one causes a decrease in demand for the other, because the goods are typically used together.

**Example:** Coffee and coffee creamer are complement goods, so a higher price of coffee tends to decrease demand for creamer.

#### Concentration Ratio

A measure of market structure calculated as the combined market share of the largest few firms, usually the top four, in an industry.

#### Congestion Pricing

A policy that charges drivers a fee for using a road or area during periods of heavy traffic, intended to reduce the negative externality of congestion by reflecting its true social cost.

**Example:** A city charging a toll for driving into its downtown core during rush hour, but not at other times, is using congestion pricing.

#### Consumer Optimum

The combination of goods a consumer chooses that maximizes total satisfaction given the limits of the budget constraint.

**Example:** A consumer's optimum might be 4 apples and 3 oranges, the affordable bundle that leaves her as satisfied as possible.

#### Consumer Surplus

The difference between the maximum price a buyer is willing to pay for a good and the price actually paid, summed across all buyers.

**Example:** If a buyer would have paid up to $10 for a shirt but only pays $7, she gains $3 of consumer surplus.

#### Coordination Game

A game in which players benefit most by choosing matching strategies, so multiple Nash equilibria exist and the main challenge is agreeing on which one to settle on.

#### Cournot Best-Response Function

A rule showing the profit-maximizing quantity for one firm to produce, given a specific quantity choice by its rival, in the Cournot model.

#### Cournot Model

A model of oligopoly in which firms simultaneously choose the quantities they will produce, and the market price adjusts based on total output.

**Example:** In the Cournot model, two firms each choose how many units to produce, and the combined total determines the price both receive.

#### Cournot-Nash Equilibrium

The pair of quantities in the Cournot model at which each firm is producing its best response to the other firm's quantity, so neither wants to change output unilaterally.

**Example:** At the Cournot-Nash equilibrium, both firms are satisfied with their output choices given what the other is producing, even though total output is higher than under collusion.

#### Credit Rationing

A situation in which lenders limit the amount of credit extended to borrowers, or deny loans altogether, even at the prevailing interest rate, because raising the rate further would attract disproportionately riskier borrowers.

#### Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand

A measure of how much the quantity demanded of one good changes in percentage terms in response to a one-percent change in the price of a different good.

**Example:** A positive cross-price elasticity between tea and coffee confirms they are substitutes, since a higher coffee price raises demand for tea.

#### Current Account

The component of the balance of payments that records a country's trade in goods and services, along with net income earned from foreign investments and net transfer payments, but excluding financial asset transactions.

#### Deadweight Loss

The loss of total surplus to society that occurs when a market produces less than the efficient quantity, often because price exceeds marginal cost.

**Example:** Monopoly pricing creates a deadweight loss because some mutually beneficial trades between willing buyers and the firm's low production cost never happen.

#### Demand

The willingness and ability of buyers to purchase different quantities of a good at different prices during a given time period.

**Example:** A consumer's demand for coffee describes how many cups she would buy at $2 each, at $4 each, and so on.

#### Demand Curve

A graph showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity of it that buyers wish to purchase, holding other factors constant, typically sloping downward.

**Example:** Plotting the price of coffee against cups purchased per week traces out a downward-sloping demand curve.

#### Demand Curve Derivation

The process of building an individual demand curve by tracing out the quantity of a good a consumer chooses at each possible price, holding income and other prices constant.

#### Derived Demand

The demand for an input, such as labor, that arises from and depends on the demand for the final good or service that input helps produce.

#### Determinants of Demand

The factors other than a good's own price that influence how much of it buyers wish to purchase, including income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations, and the number of buyers.

**Example:** A rise in consumer income is a determinant of demand that can shift the entire demand curve for restaurant meals.

#### Determinants of Price Elasticity

The factors that make demand for a good more or less responsive to price changes, including availability of substitutes, the share of income spent on the good, whether it is a necessity or luxury, and the length of the time period considered.

#### Determinants of Supply

Factors other than a good's own price that influence how much of it sellers wish to offer, including input costs, technology, expectations, the number of sellers, and prices of related goods in production.

**Example:** A new fertilizer that raises crop yields is a determinant of supply that can shift the entire supply curve for wheat.

#### Diminishing Marginal Returns

The tendency for the marginal product of a variable input to eventually decline as more of it is added to a fixed amount of other inputs.

**Example:** Adding more and more workers to a single kitchen eventually produces smaller and smaller increases in meals served, as workers get in each other's way.

#### Diminishing Marginal Utility

The tendency for the additional satisfaction gained from each successive unit of a good to decline as more of it is consumed.

**Example:** The first slice of pizza is highly satisfying, but by the fifth slice the added enjoyment is much smaller, illustrating diminishing marginal utility.

#### Diseconomies of Scale

A situation in which long-run average cost rises as a firm increases the scale of its output, often due to coordination and management difficulties in very large organizations.

**Example:** A company that grows so large that layers of management slow decision-making and raise costs per unit is experiencing diseconomies of scale.

#### Dominant Strategy

A strategy that gives a player the best outcome no matter what strategy the other player chooses.

**Example:** If a firm earns more profit by advertising whether or not its rival advertises, advertising is the firm's dominant strategy.

#### Dominated Strategy

A strategy that gives a player a worse outcome than some other available strategy, regardless of what the other player chooses, and so would never be rationally selected.

#### Economic Model

A simplified representation of economic relationships, often using graphs or equations, built to explain or predict behavior by focusing on the most important factors.

#### Economic Profit

The difference between a firm's total revenue and its total costs, including both explicit costs and the implicit opportunity cost of resources the owner supplies, such as forgone wages.

**Example:** A business owner earning $50,000 in accounting profit but who gave up a $60,000 salaried job to run the business actually has an economic loss of $10,000.

#### Economic Rent

A payment to the owner of a resource, such as land, that is in fixed or nearly fixed supply, in excess of the minimum amount needed to keep that resource in its current use.

**Example:** Because the total supply of a scenic downtown lot is essentially fixed, much of the high price a business pays to use it is economic rent rather than a cost of producing more of it.

#### Economies of Scale

A situation in which long-run average cost falls as a firm increases the scale of its output.

**Example:** A car manufacturer that spreads the cost of expensive machinery over a much larger number of vehicles experiences economies of scale.

#### Education Signaling Model

A model in which workers acquire education not necessarily because it increases their productivity, but because it credibly signals existing ability to employers who cannot directly observe that ability.

**Example:** In the education signaling model, a high-ability worker earns a degree mainly to prove to employers, at relatively low personal cost, an ability that lower-ability workers would find too costly to fake.

#### Elastic Demand

A condition in which quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than the percentage change in price, so price elasticity of demand is greater than one in absolute value.

**Example:** Demand for a specific brand of soda is often elastic, since buyers can easily switch to another brand if its price rises.

#### Elasticity

A general measure of how responsive one economic variable, such as quantity, is to a change in another variable, such as price or income, expressed as a ratio of percentage changes.

#### Endowment Effect

The tendency for people to value something more highly simply because they already own it, compared to the price they would pay to acquire the identical item.

**Example:** A person who wouldn't pay $20 for a mug but refuses to sell an identical mug they already own for less than $30 is exhibiting the endowment effect.

#### Equal Marginal Principle

The rule that a consumer with a limited budget maximizes total utility by allocating spending so that the marginal utility per dollar spent is equal across all goods purchased.

**Example:** A consumer stops buying more apples and starts buying oranges once a dollar spent on oranges yields as much extra satisfaction as a dollar spent on apples.

#### Equilibrium Price

The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied in a market, found where the demand and supply curves intersect.

**Example:** If the demand and supply curves for wheat cross at $6 per bushel, that is the equilibrium price.

#### Equilibrium Quantity

The amount of a good bought and sold at the equilibrium price, where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

#### Excess Capacity

In monopolistic competition, the gap between a firm's actual long-run output and the lower-cost output level it would produce at the minimum point of its average total cost curve.

**Example:** A monopolistically competitive restaurant operating at excess capacity could serve more customers at a lower average cost per meal, but demand for its differentiated menu isn't high enough to fill all those seats.

#### Excludability

A characteristic of a good describing whether it is possible to prevent people who have not paid for it from consuming it.

#### Externality

A cost or benefit of an economic activity that falls on a third party who did not choose to be involved in that activity, and so is not reflected in the market price.

**Example:** Secondhand smoke affecting a bystander who did not choose to smoke is an externality of smoking.

#### Externality Deadweight Loss

The loss of total surplus to society that results when a market with an externality produces a quantity different from the socially efficient quantity.

**Example:** Because a polluting factory ignores the external cost of its emissions and overproduces, the resulting externality deadweight loss represents harmful units whose social cost exceeds their social benefit.

#### Factor Endowment

The quantity of productive resources, such as land, labor, and capital, that a country possesses, which can shape the pattern of goods it produces and trades.

#### Factor-Price Equalization

A prediction of trade theory that, as countries trade freely, the prices of identical productive factors, such as the wage paid to similar workers, will tend to converge across trading countries.

**Example:** Factor-price equalization suggests that wages for similarly skilled workers in two freely trading countries should move closer together over time, even without any direct migration of workers between them.

#### First-Degree Discrimination

A form of price discrimination in which a seller charges each individual buyer the maximum price that buyer is willing to pay, capturing all consumer surplus as profit.

**Example:** A car salesperson who negotiates a separate, maximum price with each customer individually is attempting first-degree discrimination.

#### Fixed Cost

A cost of production that does not change with the quantity of output produced in the short run.

**Example:** A factory's rent payment is a fixed cost because it must be paid whether the factory produces 100 units or zero units that month.

#### Framing Effect

The tendency for people's decisions to be influenced by how choices are presented or described, even when the underlying options are objectively identical.

#### Free-Rider Problem

The tendency for individuals to benefit from a non-excludable good, such as a public good, without contributing to its cost, leading to underfunding relative to the good's true social value.

**Example:** Residents who enjoy a city's fireworks display without donating to help pay for it illustrate the free-rider problem.

#### Gains From Trade

The increase in total consumption or welfare that trading countries, or individuals, can achieve by specializing according to comparative advantage and exchanging goods, compared to producing everything themselves.

**Example:** Two countries that each specialize and trade can together consume more goods in total than if each had tried to produce everything it needed on its own, a demonstration of the gains from trade.

#### Game

A formal situation of strategic interaction in which the outcome for each participant, called a player, depends on the choices made by all the players involved.

**Example:** Two gas stations deciding simultaneously whether to charge high or low prices, with profits depending on both decisions, form a game.

#### Giffen Good

A good for which quantity demanded falls as its price falls, because a strong negative income effect for this inferior good outweighs the normal substitution effect.

**Example:** A historically cited possible case is a staple food so central to a very poor household's diet that a lower price frees up income that gets spent on more varied foods, reducing purchases of the staple itself.

#### Globalization

The increasing economic integration and interdependence of countries worldwide through growing trade, investment, migration, and the spread of technology and information.

**Example:** A clothing brand designing its products in one country, manufacturing them in another, and selling them worldwide is an everyday illustration of globalization.

#### Government Failure

A situation in which government intervention intended to correct a market failure instead makes resource allocation less efficient, due to factors like poor information, political incentives, or unintended side effects.

**Example:** A well-intentioned subsidy that ends up being captured mostly by lobbyists and large firms rather than helping its intended beneficiaries illustrates government failure.

#### Heckscher-Ohlin Model

A model of international trade predicting that countries will export goods that rely heavily on the production factors they have in relative abundance, and import goods that rely on factors they have relatively less of.

**Example:** The Heckscher-Ohlin model predicts that a capital-abundant country will tend to export capital-intensive manufactured goods while importing labor-intensive goods.

#### Herfindahl-Hirschman Index

A measure of market concentration calculated by summing the squares of each firm's percentage market share in an industry, giving more weight to larger firms than a simple concentration ratio.

**Example:** An industry with four equal firms, each holding 25% market share, has a Herfindahl-Hirschman Index of 2,500, calculated as four times 25 squared.

#### Heuristic

A mental shortcut or simple rule of thumb that people use to make decisions quickly, which often works well but can sometimes lead to systematic errors.

#### Human Capital

The accumulated skills, knowledge, training, and experience that make a worker more productive and thus more valuable in the labor market.

#### Hyperbolic Discounting

A pattern of valuing the future in which the implied rate of impatience is much higher for choices between the present and the near future than for choices between two points further in the future.

**Example:** A person might strongly prefer $100 today over $110 in one month, yet prefer waiting an extra month for $110 over $100 if both payments are a year away, a pattern consistent with hyperbolic discounting.

#### Import Quota

A government-imposed limit on the physical quantity of a good that may be imported into a country during a given period.

#### Incentive Compatibility

A property of a contract or mechanism in which it is in each participant's own self-interest to act in the way the designer intends, given their private information.

**Example:** A sales commission structure is incentive compatible if it makes it in a salesperson's own financial interest to put in genuine effort rather than slack off.

#### Income Effect

The change in quantity demanded of a good that results from a change in a consumer's purchasing power caused by a price change, holding relative prices constant.

**Example:** When the price of gasoline falls, a driver's effective purchasing power rises, and the resulting increase in driving distance partly reflects the income effect.

#### Income Elasticity of Demand

A measure of how much quantity demanded changes in percentage terms in response to a one-percent change in buyer income.

**Example:** A positive income elasticity of demand for restaurant meals confirms they are a normal good, since demand rises as income rises.

#### Increasing Opportunity Cost

The pattern in which each additional unit of one good requires giving up progressively larger amounts of another good, because resources are not equally well-suited to every use.

**Example:** As a farm shifts more land from wheat to corn, it must use land less suited to corn, so each extra bushel of corn costs more bushels of wheat than the one before.

#### Indifference Curve

A graph showing all combinations of two goods that provide a consumer with the same total level of satisfaction.

**Example:** A bundle of 6 apples and 2 oranges and a bundle of 3 apples and 5 oranges might lie on the same indifference curve if a consumer values both equally.

#### Individual Transferable Quota

A tradable permit granting its holder the right to harvest a specific quantity of a common-pool resource, such as fish, designed to limit overall use while allowing efficient reallocation among users.

#### Inelastic Demand

A condition in which quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than the percentage change in price, so price elasticity of demand is less than one in absolute value.

**Example:** Demand for insulin tends to be inelastic, because patients who need it keep buying it even if the price rises significantly.

#### Inequity Aversion

A specific social preference in which people dislike outcomes that are unequal between themselves and others, even when they could gain personally by accepting the unequal outcome.

**Example:** Rejecting an offer to receive $20 out of $100 while the other person keeps $80, even though $20 is better than nothing, reflects inequity aversion.

#### Infant Industry Argument

The argument that a new domestic industry, not yet able to compete with established foreign producers, deserves temporary protection from import competition until it becomes efficient enough to compete on its own.

**Example:** A developing country might temporarily tax imported cars under the infant industry argument, giving its fledgling domestic auto industry time to grow before facing full foreign competition.

#### Inferior Good

A good for which quantity demanded falls when buyer income rises, and rises when income falls, holding price constant.

**Example:** Instant noodles may be an inferior good if people buy less of them once they can afford pricier meals as their income rises.

#### Information Asymmetry

A situation in which one party to a transaction has more or better information relevant to that transaction than the other party.

#### Informative Advertising

Advertising that provides buyers with useful facts about a product's price, features, or availability, helping them make better-informed purchasing decisions.

#### Interest Rate

The price paid, typically expressed as a percentage per year, for borrowing funds or the return earned for lending or saving funds.

#### Kinked Demand Curve

A model of oligopoly pricing in which a firm believes rivals will match any price cut but ignore any price increase, creating a demand curve with a sharp bend at the current price that discourages price changes.

**Example:** The kinked demand curve model helps explain why oligopoly prices can stay unusually stable even when costs shift slightly.

#### Labor Demand Curve

A graph showing the quantity of labor a firm or industry wishes to hire at each possible wage rate, which slopes downward because marginal revenue product typically declines as more workers are hired.

#### Labor Hiring Rule

The profit-maximizing rule that a firm should hire additional workers up to the point where the marginal revenue product of labor equals the wage rate.

**Example:** A firm stops hiring once the next worker's marginal revenue product would be only $12 but the wage rate is $15, since that hire would reduce profit.

#### Labor Market Equilibrium

The wage rate and quantity of labor at which the quantity of labor demanded by firms equals the quantity of labor supplied by workers.

**Example:** Labor market equilibrium for retail workers in a city might occur at a wage of $16 per hour, where the number of workers willing to work matches the number firms wish to hire.

#### Labor Supply Income Effect

The tendency for a higher wage to raise a worker's overall income, which can lead the worker to want more leisure and so supply fewer hours of labor.

**Example:** A worker who gets a large raise might choose to work fewer hours because the income effect makes the worker feel they can now afford more leisure time.

#### Labor Supply Substitution Effect

The tendency for a higher wage to make leisure more costly relative to work, encouraging a worker to supply more hours of labor.

**Example:** A rise in the hourly wage can make a worker's current leisure time feel more expensive to keep, pushing them, through the substitution effect, toward working more hours.

#### Law of Demand

The principle that, holding other factors constant, the quantity demanded of a good falls as its price rises, and rises as its price falls.

**Example:** When the price of movie tickets increases, fewer people choose to go to the movies, all else equal.

#### Law of Supply

The principle that, holding other factors constant, the quantity supplied of a good rises as its price rises, and falls as its price falls.

#### Libertarian Paternalism

A policy philosophy that favors using nudges to steer people toward decisions that are better for themselves, while preserving their freedom to choose a different option if they wish.

**Example:** Placing healthier food options at eye level in a cafeteria, while still offering less healthy options elsewhere on the menu, reflects libertarian paternalism.

#### Loanable Funds Market

A market in which savers supply funds and borrowers demand funds, with the interest rate adjusting to balance the quantity of funds supplied with the quantity demanded.

**Example:** In the loanable funds market, a rise in household saving increases the supply of funds available for businesses to borrow, putting downward pressure on the interest rate.

#### Long Run

A time period long enough that a firm can adjust all of its inputs, including fixed inputs like plant size, and can enter or exit an industry entirely.

#### Long-Run Average Cost

The lowest possible average total cost of producing each quantity of output when a firm is free to choose the optimal size of all its inputs, including plant size.

**Example:** A firm's long-run average cost curve traces the cheapest way to produce 1,000 units, 2,000 units, and so on, after choosing the best-sized factory for each quantity.

#### Long-Run Competitive Equilibrium

The outcome in a perfectly competitive market, after entry and exit have run their course, in which price equals the minimum long-run average cost and firms earn zero economic profit.

**Example:** In long-run competitive equilibrium, new firms stop entering the wheat market because existing farmers are earning only enough to cover all their costs, including opportunity costs.

#### Loss Aversion

The tendency for people to feel the pain of a loss more strongly than the pleasure of an equivalent gain.

**Example:** An investor who refuses to sell a stock at a small loss, even when the money could be better used elsewhere, is often displaying loss aversion.

#### Marginal Abatement Cost

The additional cost of reducing pollution or another harmful activity by one more unit, typically rising as more pollution is eliminated.

**Example:** A factory's marginal abatement cost rises from $10 to $50 per ton of emissions removed as it cuts pollution closer to zero.

#### Marginal Analysis

A method of decision-making that compares the additional benefit of an action to its additional cost, rather than looking only at total benefits and costs.

**Example:** A firm deciding whether to produce one more unit checks whether the revenue from that unit exceeds the cost of making it.

#### Marginal Benefit

The additional satisfaction, revenue, or value gained from one more unit of an activity, good, or service.

**Example:** The marginal benefit of eating a third slice of pizza is the extra enjoyment it provides, which is usually less than the enjoyment from the first slice.

#### Marginal Cost

The additional cost incurred from producing or consuming one more unit of a good or activity.

**Example:** If making one more loaf of bread requires $2 of extra flour and labor, the marginal cost of that loaf is $2.

#### Marginal Cost of Labor

The additional total labor cost a monopsony employer incurs from hiring one more worker, which exceeds the wage paid to that worker because hiring more workers requires raising the wage paid to all workers.

**Example:** If a monopsony must raise its wage from $14 to $15 per hour to attract one more worker, and it already employs 100 workers, the marginal cost of that extra worker is well above $15 because the raise applies to everyone already employed.

#### Marginal External Benefit

The additional benefit received by third parties, beyond the buyer, from one more unit of a good being consumed.

#### Marginal External Cost

The additional cost imposed on third parties, beyond the producer and buyer, from producing one more unit of a good.

#### Marginal Product

The additional output produced by adding one more unit of a variable input, holding other inputs constant.

**Example:** If hiring a fourth worker raises a farm's output from 150 to 180 bushels, the marginal product of that worker is 30 bushels.

#### Marginal Rate of Substitution

The rate at which a consumer is willing to trade one good for another while keeping total satisfaction unchanged, measured as the slope of an indifference curve.

**Example:** A marginal rate of substitution of 2 means a consumer is willing to give up 2 apples to get 1 additional orange without feeling better or worse off.

#### Marginal Revenue

The additional revenue a firm earns from selling one more unit of output.

**Example:** A price-taking firm selling wheat at $6 per bushel earns a marginal revenue of $6 from each additional bushel sold.

#### Marginal Revenue Product

The additional revenue a firm earns by employing one more unit of an input, calculated as the input's marginal product multiplied by the marginal revenue from selling the extra output.

**Example:** If an additional worker produces 10 extra units and each unit sells for $5, that worker's marginal revenue product is $50.

#### Marginal Utility

The additional satisfaction a consumer gains from consuming one more unit of a good, holding consumption of other goods constant.

**Example:** The marginal utility of a fourth slice of pizza is the extra satisfaction gained beyond what the first three slices already provided.

#### Marginal-Cost Pricing Regulation

A regulatory approach that requires a monopoly, especially a natural monopoly, to set its price equal to its marginal cost, achieving allocative efficiency but potentially causing financial losses.

#### Market

An arrangement, physical or virtual, through which buyers and sellers interact to exchange goods, services, or resources.

**Example:** A farmers market, a stock exchange, and an app that connects riders and drivers are all markets.

#### Market Equilibrium

The price and quantity at which the quantity buyers wish to purchase exactly equals the quantity sellers wish to offer, so there is no tendency for price to change.

**Example:** In the coffee market, equilibrium might occur at a price of $3 per cup, where both buyers and sellers are satisfied trading 200 cups.

#### Market Failure

A situation in which a market, left on its own, fails to allocate resources efficiently, often due to externalities, public goods, information problems, or market power.

**Example:** Pollution from a factory that harms nearby residents without the factory bearing that cost is a classic case of market failure.

#### Market for Lemons

A model describing how severe information asymmetry about quality can cause high-quality goods to be driven out of a market, leaving mainly low-quality ("lemon") goods for sale.

**Example:** In the classic market-for-lemons scenario, used car buyers who cannot tell good cars from bad ones offer only an average price, discouraging owners of genuinely good used cars from selling.

#### Market Power as Failure

The classification of market power, such as that held by a monopoly, as a form of market failure because it leads the firm to restrict output below the efficient level and charge a price above marginal cost.

**Example:** A monopoly's ability to set a price higher than the competitive level illustrates market power as failure, since it leaves some beneficial trades unmade.

#### Market Shortage

A situation in which, at the current price, the quantity buyers wish to purchase exceeds the quantity sellers wish to supply, typically occurring when price is below equilibrium.

**Example:** When a popular concert sets ticket prices too low, demand for tickets outstrips the number available, creating a shortage.

#### Market Surplus

A situation in which, at the current price, the quantity sellers wish to supply exceeds the quantity buyers wish to purchase, typically occurring when price is above equilibrium.

**Example:** If a store prices winter coats too high in spring, unsold coats piling up on the racks reflect a market surplus.

#### Markup Over Marginal Cost

The amount by which a firm with market power sets its price above its marginal cost, reflecting the downward-sloping demand curve it faces for its differentiated product.

**Example:** A firm with a marginal cost of $4 per unit that charges $6 has a markup over marginal cost of $2.

#### Median Voter Theorem

The proposition that, in a majority-rule election between two candidates, the outcome tends to reflect the preference of the voter positioned exactly in the middle of the distribution of voter preferences.

**Example:** The median voter theorem helps explain why two competing political candidates often adopt surprisingly similar moderate positions on public spending.

#### Mental Accounting

The tendency for people to mentally sort money into separate categories, such as a vacation fund or a grocery budget, and treat money in each category differently, even though money is fully interchangeable.

**Example:** A person who refuses to touch "vacation savings" to cover an overdue bill, while leaving an equal amount idle in a low-interest checking account, is practicing mental accounting.

#### Midpoint Elasticity Formula

A method for calculating price elasticity of demand that divides percentage changes in quantity and price by the average of the initial and final values, giving the same result whether price rises or falls.

#### Minimum Efficient Scale

The smallest level of output at which a firm achieves the lowest possible long-run average cost.

#### Monopolist Marginal Revenue

The additional revenue a monopolist earns from selling one more unit, which is less than the price charged because selling more requires lowering the price on all units sold.

**Example:** A monopolist's marginal revenue from an extra unit might be only $3, even though it sells that unit for $8, because selling the extra unit forced the price down on other units too.

#### Monopolistic Competition

A market structure with many sellers offering similar but not identical products, free entry and exit, giving each firm some control over its own price.

**Example:** Restaurants in a city compete under monopolistic competition, since each offers a somewhat different dining experience but faces many rival establishments.

#### Monopolistic Short-Run Profit

The positive economic profit a firm in monopolistic competition can earn temporarily when price exceeds average total cost, before new competitors enter the market.

#### Monopolistic Zero Profit

The long-run outcome in monopolistic competition in which entry of new firms, drawn by short-run profits, continues until each existing firm's price equals its average total cost and economic profit falls to zero.

**Example:** As new coffee shops keep opening in a profitable neighborhood, each existing shop's share of customers shrinks until the market reaches monopolistic zero profit.

#### Monopoly

A market structure in which a single firm is the only seller of a good or service that has no close substitutes, giving that firm substantial control over price.

**Example:** A local water utility that is the sole provider of piped water to a city operates as a monopoly.

#### Monopoly Profit Maximization

The process by which a monopolist selects the output level where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, then charges the highest price buyers will pay for that quantity according to the demand curve.

**Example:** A monopolist might determine that producing 1,000 units maximizes profit, then set the price at the level on the demand curve corresponding to that quantity.

#### Monopsony

A market structure in which there is only one buyer of a particular input, such as labor, giving that buyer power to influence the price it pays.

**Example:** A single large factory that is the only major employer in a small town acts as a monopsony in the local labor market.

#### Monopsony Hiring Rule

The profit-maximizing rule that a monopsony employer hires workers up to the point where the marginal revenue product of labor equals the marginal cost of labor, resulting in a lower wage and fewer workers hired than in a competitive labor market.

**Example:** Following the monopsony hiring rule, a sole factory employer in town hires fewer workers at a lower wage than would occur if several factories were competing for the same workers.

#### Moral Hazard

A problem arising from information asymmetry in which one party changes their behavior to take on more risk after a transaction, because the other party bears some of the consequences.

**Example:** A driver with full car insurance coverage might drive a little less carefully, knowing the insurance company will cover most of the cost of any accident, illustrating moral hazard.

#### MR Equals MC Rule

The profit-maximizing rule stating that a firm should produce the quantity of output at which marginal revenue equals marginal cost.

**Example:** A firm stops increasing output once producing one more unit would cost more than it would add in revenue, which occurs where marginal revenue equals marginal cost.

#### Nash Equilibrium

A situation in a game in which every player is choosing the best strategy available given the strategies chosen by all other players, so no player can benefit by changing their own strategy alone.

**Example:** In a pricing game between two firms, a Nash equilibrium occurs where each firm's chosen price is its best response to the other firm's price.

#### Natural Monopoly

A market in which long-run average cost keeps falling over the entire relevant range of output, so a single large firm can supply the market more cheaply than multiple smaller firms could.

**Example:** A single electric grid serving a city is often considered a natural monopoly because building duplicate sets of power lines would be far more costly than one network.

#### Negative Externality

An externality in which a third party bears a cost from an economic activity, causing the market to produce more of the good than is socially efficient.

**Example:** Air pollution from a power plant that harms the health of nearby residents who have no say in the plant's operation is a negative externality.

#### Non-Credible Threat

A threatened action in a sequential game that a player would not actually find it rational to carry out if actually called upon to do so, and which rational opponents can therefore ignore.

**Example:** An incumbent firm's threat to start a costly price war against any entrant is a non-credible threat if the price war would actually hurt the incumbent more than simply tolerating the new competitor.

#### Non-Price Competition

Rivalry among firms conducted through means other than lowering price, such as advertising, product quality, customer service, or store location.

#### Normal Good

A good for which quantity demanded rises when buyer income rises, and falls when income falls, holding price constant.

**Example:** Restaurant dining is a normal good for most people, since they eat out more often as their income increases.

#### Normative Economics

The branch of economic analysis that makes value judgments about what economic outcomes or policies ought to be.

**Example:** "The government should raise the minimum wage because it is fair" is a normative statement based on values rather than facts alone.

#### Nudge

A feature of how choices are presented, such as a default option, that is designed to steer people's decisions in a particular direction without restricting any of the options actually available to them.

**Example:** Automatically enrolling new employees in a retirement savings plan, while still letting them opt out at any time, is a nudge.

#### Oligopoly

A market structure dominated by a small number of large firms whose decisions about price and output significantly affect one another.

**Example:** The commercial airplane manufacturing industry, dominated by just a couple of major producers, is an oligopoly.

#### Oligopoly Antitrust Policy

The application of competition laws specifically aimed at preventing collusion, price-fixing, and anti-competitive mergers among the small number of dominant firms in concentrated industries.

#### Opportunity Cost

The value of the next-best alternative given up when a person or society chooses one option over another.

**Example:** The opportunity cost of spending $20 on a movie ticket is the book that could have been bought with the same $20.

#### Payoff Matrix

A table used in game theory that lists the outcomes, or payoffs, each player receives for every possible combination of strategies chosen by all players.

**Example:** A payoff matrix for two competing firms might show that if both charge high prices, each earns $10 million, but if one undercuts the other, the undercutter earns more and the rival earns less.

#### Perfect Competition

A market structure characterized by many small buyers and sellers, identical products, free entry and exit, and full information, resulting in no single firm having power over price.

**Example:** Markets for many agricultural commodities, such as wheat, closely approximate perfect competition because no single farmer's output noticeably affects the market price.

#### Perfectly Elastic Demand

A condition in which buyers will purchase any amount at one specific price but none at all at any higher price, represented by a horizontal demand curve.

**Example:** A wheat farmer selling into a large world market faces nearly perfectly elastic demand, since buyers can get identical wheat elsewhere at the going price.

#### Perfectly Inelastic Demand

A condition in which quantity demanded does not change at all regardless of the price charged, represented by a vertical demand curve.

**Example:** Demand for a specific life-saving medication with no substitutes might be nearly perfectly inelastic for patients who need it.

#### Persuasive Advertising

Advertising designed to shape consumer preferences or create a perceived difference between similar products, rather than to convey factual information.

**Example:** An ad associating a soft drink with excitement and popularity, without describing the product itself, is persuasive advertising.

#### Pigouvian Subsidy

A payment given for an activity that generates a positive externality, set equal to the external benefit per unit, in order to encourage the socially efficient level of that activity.

**Example:** A government subsidy for each household that installs solar panels is a Pigouvian subsidy meant to reflect the broader environmental benefits solar power provides to others.

#### Pigouvian Tax

A tax levied on an activity that generates a negative externality, set equal to the external cost per unit, in order to align private incentives with the full social cost.

**Example:** A tax on each ton of carbon emitted is a Pigouvian tax designed to make polluters pay for the climate damage their emissions cause to others.

#### Positive Economics

The branch of economic analysis that describes and predicts factual, testable relationships between economic variables without judging whether outcomes are good or bad.

**Example:** "A minimum wage increase will reduce employment among low-skill workers by a certain percentage" is a positive statement that could be checked against data.

#### Positive Externality

An externality in which a third party receives a benefit from an economic activity, causing the market to produce less of the good than is socially efficient.

**Example:** A beekeeper's bees pollinating a neighboring farmer's crops, boosting that farmer's harvest for free, is a positive externality.

#### Present Bias

The tendency to place disproportionately greater weight on immediate costs and benefits relative to future ones, beyond what a consistent discounting of the future would justify.

**Example:** Choosing to spend a bonus on an enjoyable weekend trip now, despite having planned all year to save it for retirement, reflects present bias.

#### Present Value

The current worth of an amount of money to be received in the future, calculated by discounting it back using an interest rate to reflect that money today is worth more than the same amount later.

**Example:** At a 5% interest rate, $105 received one year from now has a present value of exactly $100 today.

#### Price Discrimination

The practice of charging different prices to different customers for the same good or service, not based on differences in the cost of serving them.

**Example:** A movie theater charging students a lower ticket price than other adults for the identical movie is practicing price discrimination.

#### Price Elasticity of Demand

A measure of how much the quantity demanded of a good changes in percentage terms in response to a one-percent change in its price.

**Example:** A price elasticity of demand of -2 means a 1% price increase causes quantity demanded to fall by about 2%.

#### Price Elasticity of Supply

A measure of how much the quantity supplied of a good changes in percentage terms in response to a one-percent change in its price.

**Example:** Supply of a perishable crop right after harvest tends to be inelastic, since farmers cannot quickly produce more of it.

#### Price Maker

A seller with enough market power to influence the price of its product by changing the quantity it offers for sale, rather than simply accepting a market price.

#### Price Taker

A buyer or seller in a market who has no ability to influence the market price and must accept the price determined by the overall market.

#### Principal-Agent Problem

A conflict that arises when one party, the principal, hires another party, the agent, to act on their behalf, but the agent's interests differ from the principal's and the principal cannot perfectly monitor the agent's actions.

**Example:** Shareholders of a company, the principals, may struggle to ensure that company executives, the agents, always act in the shareholders' best interest rather than their own.

#### Prisoners Dilemma

A game in which each player has a dominant strategy that leads to a worse outcome for both players collectively than if they had cooperated, illustrating a conflict between individual and group interests.

**Example:** Two firms that would both profit more by keeping prices high end up competing them down because each individually gains by undercutting the other, a classic prisoners' dilemma.

#### Producer Surplus

The difference between the price a seller actually receives for a good and the minimum price at which the seller would have been willing to sell it, summed across all sellers.

**Example:** If a farmer would have accepted as little as $4 per bushel but sells at $6, he gains $2 of producer surplus per bushel.

#### Product Differentiation

The practice of making a firm's product distinct from competitors' products through features, quality, branding, or style, giving buyers a reason to prefer it.

#### Product Variety

The range of differentiated versions of a good or service available to consumers in a market, often seen as a benefit of monopolistic competition despite the cost of excess capacity.

#### Production Frontier

A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce given its fixed resources and current technology.

**Example:** A frontier might show that an economy can produce 100 cars and 0 computers, 0 cars and 200 computers, or any combination along the curve between them.

#### Production Function

A relationship showing the maximum quantity of output a firm can produce from given quantities of inputs, such as labor and capital, using current technology.

**Example:** A production function might show that a bakery using 5 workers and 2 ovens can produce at most 300 loaves of bread per day.

#### Productive Efficiency

A situation in which goods are produced using the fewest possible resources, so no more of one good can be produced without producing less of another.

#### Prospect Theory

A behavioral model of decision-making under risk describing how people evaluate potential gains and losses relative to a reference point, rather than final wealth levels, and tend to weigh losses more heavily than equivalent gains.

**Example:** Prospect theory helps explain why people are often more upset about losing $50 than they are happy about gaining $50, even though the dollar amounts are identical.

#### Protectionism

A set of government policies, such as tariffs and import quotas, designed to shield domestic producers from foreign competition.

**Example:** A government that raises tariffs on imported clothing specifically to help local garment makers compete is practicing protectionism.

#### Public Good

A good that is both non-excludable and non-rival, meaning it is difficult to prevent people from using it without paying and one person's use does not reduce its availability to others.

**Example:** National defense is a public good because protecting the country does not prevent any individual citizen from also being protected, and non-payers cannot easily be excluded from that protection.

#### Quantity Demanded

The specific amount of a good that buyers are willing and able to purchase at one particular price.

#### Quota Rent

The extra profit earned by whoever holds the legal right to sell goods under an import quota, arising from the gap between the lower world price and the higher domestic price the quota creates.

**Example:** A trading company holding a license to import a limited quantity of sugar under a quota can resell it domestically at a much higher price, capturing quota rent.

#### Rent-Seeking

The effort individuals or firms spend trying to gain economic advantage, such as a monopoly privilege or favorable regulation, through political influence rather than through producing more goods or services.

**Example:** A company lobbying the government for a law that blocks competitors from entering its industry, instead of competing by lowering its own costs, is engaging in rent-seeking.

#### Repeated Game

A game in which the same players interact with each other over multiple rounds, allowing strategies to depend on the history of previous rounds.

**Example:** Two firms competing in the same market year after year are playing a repeated game, where past behavior can influence future pricing decisions.

#### Ricardian Trade Model

A model of international trade showing that countries gain by specializing in and trading goods for which they have a comparative advantage, based on differences in labor productivity across countries.

**Example:** The Ricardian trade model would predict that a country relatively more productive at growing coffee than making textiles should specialize in coffee and trade for textiles.

#### Rivalry

A characteristic of a good describing whether one person's consumption of it reduces the amount available for others to consume.

#### Samuelson Condition

The economic efficiency condition for public goods stating that the sum of every individual's marginal benefit should equal the marginal cost of providing the good, rather than any single person's marginal benefit alone.

**Example:** Deciding the efficient size of a public park requires adding up the marginal benefit every visitor gets, per the Samuelson condition, since no one visitor's benefit alone would justify the park's full cost.

#### Satisficing

A decision-making approach in which a person selects an option that is good enough to meet a minimum acceptable standard, rather than searching exhaustively for the single best possible option.

**Example:** A job applicant who accepts the first offer that meets their minimum salary requirement, rather than interviewing with every possible employer, is satisficing.

#### Scarcity

The condition in which human wants for goods and services exceed the limited resources available to satisfy them, forcing choices about how resources are used.

**Example:** A country has enough land, workers, and tools to produce only a limited amount of food and clothing, so producing more of one usually means producing less of the other.

#### Screening

An action taken by a less-informed party to induce or sort counterparts into revealing their private information, often by offering a menu of choices that different types will select differently.

**Example:** An insurance company offering both a high-premium plan with low deductibles and a low-premium plan with high deductibles is screening customers, since riskier drivers tend to choose the first option.

#### Second-Degree Discrimination

A form of price discrimination in which a seller offers different prices or terms based on the quantity purchased or the version of the product chosen, letting buyers sort themselves into price categories.

**Example:** A phone company offering cheaper per-gigabyte rates on larger data plans is practicing second-degree discrimination.

#### Separating Equilibrium

An outcome of a signaling or screening model in which different types of informed parties choose different actions, allowing the uninformed party to correctly distinguish between them.

**Example:** A separating equilibrium in the job market occurs if high-ability workers choose to get a costly degree while low-ability workers do not, letting employers tell them apart.

#### Sequential Game

A game in which players make decisions in a specific order, with later players able to observe the choices made by earlier players before acting.

**Example:** A firm deciding whether to enter a market, followed by the incumbent firm deciding how to respond, is a sequential game.

#### Sherman Antitrust Act

A United States federal law passed in 1890 that outlaws contracts, combinations, and conspiracies that restrain trade, and prohibits monopolization of a market.

**Example:** The Sherman Antitrust Act has historically been used to challenge companies accused of using unfair tactics to eliminate rivals and dominate an industry.

#### Short Run

A time period in which a firm can adjust some inputs, such as labor, but at least one input, such as factory size, is fixed and cannot be changed.

#### Short-Run Firm Supply Curve

The portion of a competitive firm's short-run marginal cost curve that lies above its average variable cost curve, showing the quantity it will supply at each price.

#### Short-Run Industry Supply Curve

The sum of the quantities supplied by all individual firms in a competitive industry at each possible price in the short run.

#### Short-Run Marginal Cost

The additional total cost incurred from producing one more unit of output in the short run, when at least one input is fixed.

**Example:** If producing a 101st unit raises short-run total cost from $1,500 to $1,508, the short-run marginal cost of that unit is $8.

#### Shutdown Rule

The rule that a firm should stop producing in the short run if the price it receives is less than its average variable cost, since it would lose less money by shutting down than by operating.

**Example:** A firm facing a price below its average variable cost shuts down temporarily rather than operate, because continuing to produce would lose even more than its unavoidable fixed costs.

#### Signaling

An action taken by a better-informed party to credibly reveal private information to a less-informed party, typically because the action would be too costly for someone without that favorable characteristic to take.

**Example:** Completing a difficult college degree can serve as a signal to employers of a job applicant's ability, since the degree is harder for less capable applicants to earn.

#### Social Marginal Benefit

The total additional benefit to society from one more unit of a good, equal to the private marginal benefit to the buyer plus any marginal external benefit received by third parties.

**Example:** The social marginal benefit of a vaccination includes both the protection it gives the vaccinated person and the reduced disease risk it creates for others around them.

#### Social Marginal Cost

The total additional cost to society of producing one more unit of a good, equal to the firm's private marginal cost plus any marginal external cost imposed on third parties.

**Example:** The social marginal cost of electricity from a coal plant includes both the plant's own fuel and labor costs and the added cost of pollution damage to nearby communities.

#### Social Preferences

Preferences in which a person's well-being depends not only on their own outcomes but also on the outcomes received by others, including concerns for fairness or the welfare of others.

**Example:** A person who turns down a generous but unequal split of money in an ultimatum game, preferring no deal to one that feels unfair, is displaying social preferences.

#### Specialization

The practice of focusing production on a narrow range of goods or tasks in which a person, firm, or country has a comparative advantage.

**Example:** A bakery that focuses only on bread, while buying flour from a mill rather than growing wheat itself, is specializing.

#### Strategic Interdependence

The condition in an oligopoly in which each firm's best choice of price or output depends on the choices made by its rivals.

#### Substitute Goods

Two goods for which an increase in the price of one causes an increase in demand for the other, because buyers can use either to satisfy the same need.

**Example:** Butter and margarine are substitute goods, so a higher price of butter tends to increase demand for margarine.

#### Substitution Effect

The change in quantity demanded of a good that results from a change in its price relative to other goods, holding the consumer's satisfaction level constant.

**Example:** When the price of beef rises relative to chicken, consumers who buy more chicken instead of beef are responding to the substitution effect.

#### Sunk Cost

A cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered, and so should not affect a decision about what to do next.

**Example:** Money already spent on a non-refundable concert ticket should not influence whether to attend if a better opportunity comes up, since that money is gone either way.

#### Supply

The willingness and ability of sellers to offer different quantities of a good for sale at different prices during a given time period.

**Example:** A farmer's supply of wheat describes how many bushels he would sell at $5 per bushel, at $8 per bushel, and so on.

#### Supply Curve

A graph showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity of it that sellers wish to offer for sale, holding other factors constant, typically sloping upward.

**Example:** Plotting the price of wheat against bushels offered for sale traces out an upward-sloping supply curve.

#### Tangency Condition

The requirement that, at the consumer optimum, the indifference curve just touches the budget line without crossing it, so the marginal rate of substitution equals the ratio of the two goods' prices.

#### Tariff

A tax imposed by a government on imported goods, typically raising their price to domestic consumers and encouraging purchase of domestically produced substitutes.

**Example:** A 10% tariff on imported steel raises the price foreign steel sells for domestically, making locally produced steel comparatively cheaper.

#### Tariff Welfare Loss

The reduction in a country's total surplus that results from a tariff distorting trade away from the efficient quantity of imports, beyond any revenue the tariff raises for the government.

**Example:** Even after accounting for the tariff revenue collected by the government, the tariff welfare loss reflects the mutually beneficial trades between domestic buyers and foreign sellers that no longer occur.

#### Tax Incidence

The division of the actual economic burden of a tax between buyers and sellers, which depends on the relative price elasticities of supply and demand rather than on who physically pays the tax.

**Example:** Even if a tax on cigarettes is collected from sellers, tax incidence analysis shows that smokers bear most of the burden through higher prices because demand for cigarettes is relatively inelastic.

#### Terms of Trade

The rate at which a country can exchange its exported goods for imported goods, usually expressed as the ratio of export prices to import prices.

**Example:** If a country can trade one unit of its export for two units of an imported good, rather than only one and a half units as before, its terms of trade have improved.

#### Third-Degree Discrimination

A form of price discrimination in which a seller charges different prices to distinct groups of customers identified by an observable characteristic, such as age or location.

**Example:** Airlines charging different fares for business travelers versus leisure travelers who book well in advance are practicing third-degree discrimination.

#### Time Inconsistency

A conflict between a person's earlier plans and their later actual choices, in which preferences formed in advance are abandoned once the relevant moment actually arrives.

**Example:** Planning on Sunday to exercise every morning that week, but repeatedly skipping the gym once each morning actually arrives, illustrates time inconsistency.

#### Tit-for-Tat Strategy

A strategy in a repeated game in which a player cooperates on the first round and then mimics whatever the other player did in the previous round.

**Example:** A firm using a tit-for-tat strategy keeps prices high as long as its rival does, but immediately cuts its own price the round after the rival cuts theirs.

#### Total Cost

The sum of all fixed and variable costs incurred in producing a given quantity of output.

#### Total Product

The total quantity of output a firm produces using a given amount of inputs, especially as the quantity of one variable input changes.

**Example:** A farm's total product might rise from 100 to 180 bushels of corn when the number of workers hired rises from 2 to 4.

#### Total Revenue Test

A method for determining whether demand is elastic or inelastic over a price range by observing whether total revenue rises or falls as price changes.

**Example:** If raising the price of a good causes total revenue to fall, the total revenue test indicates that demand is elastic in that range.

#### Total Surplus

The combined consumer surplus and producer surplus generated in a market, used as a measure of the overall gains from trade.

#### Total Utility

The overall satisfaction a consumer receives from consuming a given quantity of a good or combination of goods.

#### Trade Adjustment Assistance

A government program that provides financial aid, retraining, or other support to workers and firms who are harmed by increased competition from imports.

#### Trade Deficit

The amount by which the value of a country's imported goods and services exceeds the value of its exported goods and services during a given period.

**Example:** If a country imports $500 billion worth of goods and services but exports only $400 billion, it runs a $100 billion trade deficit.

#### Trade Winners and Losers

The recognition that, while international trade increases a country's total gains overall, specific groups within that country, such as workers in import-competing industries, can be made worse off even as others benefit.

**Example:** Opening up to cheaper furniture imports can benefit consumers and import-business owners while making trade winners and losers out of domestic furniture factory workers who lose their jobs.

#### Tragedy of the Commons

The tendency for a shared, non-excludable resource that is rival in consumption to be overused and depleted because individual users bear only part of the cost of their own use.

**Example:** Overgrazing of a village's shared pasture occurs because each herder gains the full benefit of grazing more animals while sharing the cost of a depleted pasture with everyone else.

#### Unit-Elastic Demand

A condition in which the percentage change in quantity demanded exactly equals the percentage change in price, so price elasticity of demand equals exactly one in absolute value.

#### Utility

The satisfaction or well-being a person derives from consuming a good or service, used as a theoretical measure of consumer preference.

**Example:** Economists say a consumer gets utility from eating a meal, even though utility itself cannot be directly measured in physical units.

#### Variable Cost

A cost of production that changes with the quantity of output produced.

**Example:** The cost of raw materials used in manufacturing rises and falls with the number of units a factory produces, making it a variable cost.

#### Work-Leisure Trade-off

The choice individuals face between spending time working for income and keeping time as leisure, given a limited number of hours in a day.

#### World Trade Organization

An international organization that sets and enforces rules for trade between member countries, provides a forum for negotiating trade agreements, and resolves trade disputes between members.

**Example:** When one member country believes another has imposed an illegal tariff, it can bring a formal complaint to the World Trade Organization for resolution.
