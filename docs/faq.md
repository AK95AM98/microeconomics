# ECON 1101: Principles of Microeconomics FAQ

## Getting Started Questions

### What is ECON 1101 about, and who is it designed for?

ECON 1101, Principles of Microeconomics, is an introductory course taught by Prof. Sharat Batra at the University of Minnesota. It studies how individuals, households, and firms make decisions in the face of scarcity, and how those decisions interact through markets to determine prices, output, and the allocation of resources. The course is designed for college undergraduates, typically first- or second-year students, and assumes no prior economics coursework. You will learn the core toolkit economists use to analyze everyday and business decisions: scarcity and opportunity cost, supply and demand, consumer and producer behavior, market structures from perfect competition to monopoly, strategic (game-theoretic) reasoning, factor markets like labor, and the causes and remedies of market failure. For example, the course will equip you to explain not just that gasoline prices rose, but why, using the same demand-and-supply framework that applies to concert tickets, rental apartments, or coffee beans. The course closes with two "frontiers" units, behavioral economics and international trade, that connect classical theory to real human behavior and to the global economy. By the end, you will be able to read a supply-and-demand graph, reason through a strategic business decision, and evaluate a public policy proposal using the same analytical tools professional economists use. ### Do I need any prior economics background to take this course?

No. ECON 1101 is built from the ground up for students with zero prior exposure to economics. ### What math background do I need for this course?

You need basic algebra: solving linear equations, working with slopes and intercepts, reading and plotting points on a graph, and doing straightforward percentage calculations. These skills are used constantly, for instance when finding the equilibrium price and quantity where a demand equation and a supply equation are equal, or when computing a price elasticity using percentage changes in price and quantity. Calculus (derivatives) appears only in optional advanced sections of a few chapters, such as deriving marginal cost from a total cost function, and it is never required for the core learning objectives, the standard practice problems, or the quizzes. If your algebra is rusty, the main thing to review before starting is how to solve two linear equations simultaneously (substitution or setting them equal to each other), since this is exactly the skill used to find market equilibrium in Chapter 2 and is reused throughout the book. For example, if demand is Qd = 100 − 2P and supply is Qs = 20 + 3P, setting them equal and solving for P is the same algebra skill used again and again in cost curves, elasticity formulas, and factor market problems. ### What topics does this course NOT cover?

ECON 1101 is deliberately scoped to microeconomics, the behavior of individual households, firms, and markets, and it explicitly leaves several related topics to other courses. It does not cover macroeconomics, meaning GDP, inflation, unemployment, or monetary and fiscal policy; it does not cover econometrics or statistical and empirical research methods; it does not cover advanced general equilibrium theory or formal welfare theorems; and it does not cover international finance, exchange-rate economics, or financial markets and asset pricing. If you are looking for a course on how the Federal Reserve sets interest rates, why national unemployment rises and falls, or how to value a stock portfolio, those belong in a macroeconomics or finance course rather than this one. This course's international trade chapter (Chapter 16) focuses on the microeconomic side of trade, comparative advantage, tariffs, and who wins and loses from trade, rather than exchange rates or the balance of trade's macroeconomic effects. Knowing this scope upfront helps you set the right expectations: this is a course about individual decision-making and market outcomes, not about the economy-wide aggregates covered in a principles of macroeconomics course. ## Core Concepts

### What is scarcity, and why is it considered the starting point of economics?

Scarcity is the condition in which human wants for goods and services exceed the limited resources available to satisfy them, forcing choices about how those resources are used. It is the single fact that makes economics necessary at all: if resources were unlimited, there would be no need to choose, no prices, and no economic analysis required. Scarcity applies to individuals (a student has a limited number of hours in a day), firms (a factory has a limited amount of machinery and labor), and entire societies (a country has a limited amount of land, workers, and capital). For example, a country has enough resources to produce only a limited combination of food and clothing, so producing more of one usually requires producing less of the other, which is exactly what the Production Possibilities Frontier in Chapter 1 illustrates visually. Scarcity is distinct from poverty: even a wealthy society faces scarcity, because no society has literally unlimited resources relative to everyone's wants, even if its absolute level of resources is high. Nearly every concept that follows in this course, choice, opportunity cost, marginal analysis, supply and demand, is ultimately a tool for understanding how individuals and societies respond to scarcity. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics](chapters/01-introduction-to-microeconomics/index.md)

### What is the difference between choice and opportunity cost?

Choice is simply the decision to use a limited resource, such as time or money, for one purpose instead of another available purpose; it is the act of selecting among alternatives. Opportunity cost is what that choice costs you: specifically, the value of the next-best alternative you give up by making it. Every choice has an opportunity cost, but the two concepts answer different questions. "Choice" answers "what did you pick?" while "opportunity cost" answers "what did picking it cost you in terms of the next-best thing you gave up?" For example, choosing to spend $20 on a movie ticket is the choice; the opportunity cost of that choice is whatever else you valued most that $20 could have bought, such as a book you didn't buy. Opportunity costs are often hidden because they rarely appear on a receipt: the true opportunity cost of attending college includes not just tuition, but the wages you forwent by not working full time instead. Economists emphasize opportunity cost because comparing only the dollar price of options, while ignoring what else was given up, leads to systematically poor decisions. Recognizing both concepts together, and distinguishing a choice from its cost, is the foundation for every later tool in the course, especially marginal analysis. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics](chapters/01-introduction-to-microeconomics/index.md)

### What does the law of demand say, and why does it usually hold?

The law of demand states that, holding other factors constant, the quantity demanded of a good falls as its price rises, and rises as its price falls. In other words, demand curves slope downward. This usually holds for two related reasons. First, the substitution effect: as a good becomes relatively more expensive, buyers switch toward cheaper substitutes that satisfy a similar need. Second, the income effect: a higher price reduces a buyer's effective purchasing power, leading them to buy less of many goods, including this one, in most cases. For example, when the price of movie tickets rises, some moviegoers switch to streaming at home (substitution), and others simply attend fewer movies overall because their budget feels tighter (income effect). The law of demand is considered one of the most reliable empirical regularities in economics, holding across an enormous range of goods and markets, though a theoretical exception exists for Giffen goods, where a strong negative income effect for a very important inferior good can outweigh the substitution effect. Understanding why the law of demand holds, rather than simply memorizing that demand curves slope downward, is what allows you to correctly predict what happens when income, not price, changes instead. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What distinguishes a firm's short run from its long run?

The short run is a time period in which a firm can adjust some inputs, such as how many workers it hires or how many hours it operates, but at least one input, typically plant size or heavy capital equipment, is fixed and cannot be changed. The long run is a time period long enough that the firm can adjust all of its inputs, including fixed ones like plant size, and can even enter or exit the industry entirely. The distinction is not about a fixed amount of calendar time; it depends on the specific input and industry. For a small bakery, "long run" might mean a few months, since leasing a bigger oven is relatively quick; for a semiconductor fabrication plant, the long run might span several years, since building new capacity takes that long. This distinction matters because a firm's cost structure, and therefore its optimal decisions, look different in each period. In the short run, fixed costs must be paid no matter what, which is why the shutdown rule in Chapter 6 only compares price to average variable cost. In the long run, every cost becomes effectively variable, because the firm could in principle avoid it entirely by exiting, which is why long-run equilibrium in perfect competition drives economic profit to zero. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 5: Production and Costs](chapters/05-production-and-costs/index.md)

### What is market failure, and what are its main categories?

Market failure is a situation in which a market, left on its own, fails to allocate resources efficiently, meaning it does not produce the quantity of a good or service that maximizes total social welfare. The course covers three broad categories. The first is externalities, costs or benefits of an activity that fall on third parties not involved in the transaction, such as pollution from a factory harming nearby residents who had no say in the decision. The second is problems with public goods and common-pool resources, which arise because some goods are hard to exclude non-payers from (leading to free-riding) or because shared resources get overused when no one owns them (the tragedy of the commons). The third is information problems, including asymmetric information that leads to adverse selection and moral hazard, where one side of a transaction knows something important that the other side does not. Market power itself, such as that held by a monopoly, is also classified as a form of market failure because it restricts output below the efficient level. For example, a factory that pollutes a river without paying for the damage is a classic market failure, since its private costs don't reflect the true social cost of its production. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 12: Market Failures](chapters/12-market-failures/index.md)

### What is marginal analysis, and why do economists say to think "at the margin"?

Marginal analysis is a method of decision-making that compares the additional benefit of one more unit of an action to its additional cost, rather than comparing only total benefits to total costs. The core decision rule is to keep doing an activity as long as its marginal benefit is at least as large as its marginal cost, and to stop once marginal cost would exceed marginal benefit. Economists emphasize thinking "at the margin" because most real decisions are not all-or-nothing; they are about whether to do a little bit more or a little bit less of something you are already doing. For example, a firm deciding whether to produce one more unit of output should not ask whether production overall is profitable, but whether that specific next unit's revenue exceeds its specific additional cost. Marginal analysis explains behavior that would look puzzling if you only considered totals: a restaurant might keep seating one more table even on a slow night, because the marginal cost of that table (a bit more food and labor) is lower than the marginal revenue it brings in, even if the restaurant's total profit for the night is low. This single idea, comparing marginal benefit to marginal cost, reappears in nearly every later chapter, from a consumer's last dollar of spending to a monopolist's last unit of output. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics](chapters/01-introduction-to-microeconomics/index.md)

### What is a market, and what conditions does it need to function well?

A market is an arrangement, physical or virtual, through which buyers and sellers interact to exchange goods, services, or resources. A farmers market, a stock exchange, and a ride-sharing app are all markets, even though they look very different, because each one connects people who want to buy with people who want to sell. For a market to allocate resources efficiently, it generally needs clear information about prices and product quality, enough buyers and sellers that no single participant can dictate the price, the ability for buyers and sellers to freely enter or exit, and property rights clear enough that trades can actually be enforced. When these conditions hold reasonably well, as they do in markets for many agricultural commodities, the price system does a remarkably good job of coordinating the decisions of thousands of independent buyers and sellers without any central planner. When one of these conditions breaks down significantly, for instance when one seller dominates the market (monopoly) or when buyers cannot verify product quality (information asymmetry), the market can fail to produce an efficient outcome, which is exactly the subject of the market-structure and market-failure units later in the course. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What is demand, and how is it different from quantity demanded?

Demand is the entire relationship describing the willingness and ability of buyers to purchase different quantities of a good at every possible price during a given time period; it is represented visually by the whole demand curve. Quantity demanded, by contrast, is a single number: the specific amount buyers want to purchase at one particular price. The distinction matters because it tells you which direction causation runs on a graph. A change in the good's own price moves you along a fixed demand curve, changing the quantity demanded, but it does not shift the curve itself. A change in anything else that affects buying behavior, such as income, tastes, the price of a related good, or buyer expectations, shifts the entire demand curve to a new position, which economists call a change in demand. For example, if the price of coffee falls and people buy more cups, that is an increase in quantity demanded, a movement along the existing demand curve. But if a health report convinces people coffee is good for them and they buy more cups at every price level, that is an increase in demand, a shift of the whole curve to the right. Keeping this distinction straight is essential for correctly reading every supply-and-demand graph in the course. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What is a demand curve, and what causes it to shift versus simply move along it?

A demand curve is a graph showing the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity of it that buyers wish to purchase, holding all other factors constant; it typically slopes downward, consistent with the law of demand. A movement along a fixed demand curve happens only when the good's own price changes, nothing else, and it changes quantity demanded without relocating the curve. A shift of the entire curve happens when one of the determinants of demand changes instead, for example a change in consumer income, tastes, the price of a substitute or complement good, buyer expectations about the future, or the number of buyers in the market. An increase in income raising demand for a normal good, or a lower price of a complementary good like coffee creamer raising demand for coffee, are both shifts, moving the whole curve rightward. Confusing a shift with a movement is one of the most common errors students make when first learning supply and demand, so it is worth double-checking, whenever a problem changes something, whether that something is the good's own price (movement) or a different variable entirely (shift). [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What is supply, and what does the law of supply say about sellers' behavior?

Supply is the willingness and ability of sellers to offer different quantities of a good for sale at different prices during a given time period, represented by the entire supply curve. The law of supply states that, holding other factors constant, the quantity supplied of a good rises as its price rises, and falls as its price falls, which is why supply curves typically slope upward. This pattern makes economic sense because higher prices make production more profitable at the margin, encouraging existing sellers to produce more and sometimes drawing new sellers into the market, while sellers facing rising marginal costs as they expand output need a higher price to justify producing additional units. For example, a wheat farmer's supply describes how many bushels he is willing to sell at $5 per bushel versus $8 per bushel, with higher prices generally inducing more bushels offered, partly because it becomes worth using less ideal land or paying overtime wages to harvest more. Just like demand, supply has determinants other than price, such as input costs, technology, and the number of sellers, and a change in one of these shifts the entire supply curve rather than simply moving along it. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What are consumer surplus and producer surplus, and how do they combine into total surplus?

Consumer surplus is the difference between the maximum price a buyer is willing to pay for a good and the price they actually pay, summed across all buyers in a market; it measures the net benefit buyers receive from being able to trade. Producer surplus is the mirror image: the difference between the price a seller actually receives and the minimum price at which they would have been willing to sell, summed across all sellers; it measures sellers' net benefit from trading. Total surplus is simply consumer surplus plus producer surplus, and it is the standard measure economists use for the overall gains from trade that a market generates. For example, if a buyer would have paid up to $10 for a shirt but pays only $7, she gains $3 of consumer surplus; if the seller would have accepted as little as $4 but receives $7, he gains $3 of producer surplus, for $6 of total surplus from that single transaction. A perfectly competitive market in equilibrium maximizes total surplus, which is exactly why economists treat deviations from that equilibrium, caused by taxes, price controls, or market power, as sources of lost efficiency, or deadweight loss. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What is the production function, and how does it connect to a firm's costs?

A production function is a relationship showing the maximum quantity of output a firm can produce from given quantities of inputs, such as labor and capital, using current technology. For example, a production function might show that a bakery using 5 workers and 2 ovens can produce at most 300 loaves of bread per day; using more workers or ovens would allow more output, up to the limits of the function. The production function is the physical, engineering-like foundation underneath a firm's cost curves: once you know how much output a given combination of inputs can produce, and you know the price of each input, you can translate that physical relationship directly into a cost relationship. If labor eventually experiences diminishing marginal returns, meaning each additional worker adds less extra output than the one before, holding capital fixed, then the firm's short-run marginal cost curve will eventually rise as output increases, because each additional unit of output requires relatively more of the increasingly less productive input. This connection between the production function and cost curves is why Chapter 5 introduces the production function first: understanding what a firm can physically produce from its inputs is the key to understanding why its costs behave the way they do, which in turn drives every market-structure model in the chapters that follow. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 5: Production and Costs](chapters/05-production-and-costs/index.md)

### What are the defining features of perfect competition?

Perfect competition is a market structure characterized by four conditions: many small buyers and sellers, so no single participant can influence the market price; identical (homogeneous) products, so buyers have no reason to prefer one seller's good over another's; free entry and exit, so firms can start up or leave the industry without significant barriers; and full information, so buyers and sellers know prices and product quality. Together, these conditions make every firm a price taker, meaning it has no ability to set its own price and must simply accept whatever the market determines. Markets for many agricultural commodities, such as wheat, closely approximate perfect competition, since no single farmer's decision to produce more or less noticeably moves the market price. Perfect competition is important less because it describes every real market perfectly, and more because it serves as the efficiency benchmark against which every other market structure in the course, monopoly, monopolistic competition, and oligopoly, is compared. In long-run competitive equilibrium, firms earn zero economic profit and price equals the minimum long-run average cost, which is also exactly the condition for allocative efficiency: price equals marginal cost, so total surplus is maximized. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### What gives a monopoly its market power, and how does that change its pricing decision compared to a competitive firm?

A monopoly is a market structure in which a single firm is the only seller of a good or service that has no close substitutes, and its market power comes from a barrier to entry that keeps rival firms out, such as a patent, control of an essential input, government licensing, or, in the case of a natural monopoly, cost conditions that make it cheapest for only one firm to serve the whole market. Because a monopolist faces the entire downward-sloping market demand curve rather than a flat, price-taking demand curve, it becomes a price maker: it can influence price by choosing how much output to produce. This changes its marginal revenue: selling one more unit requires lowering the price on all units sold, not just the extra one, so a monopolist's marginal revenue is always below its price, unlike a competitive firm whose marginal revenue equals price. The monopolist still follows the same MR = MC rule to find its profit-maximizing quantity, but then charges whatever price the demand curve indicates buyers will pay for that quantity, a price that exceeds marginal cost. For example, a local water utility that is the only provider of piped water to a city can set a price well above its marginal cost of delivering more water, something a competitive wheat farmer could never do. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 7: Monopoly](chapters/07-monopoly/index.md)

### What is monopolistic competition, and how does it differ from both perfect competition and monopoly?

Monopolistic competition is a market structure with many sellers offering similar but not identical products, free entry and exit, and each firm having some control over its own price because of product differentiation. It borrows features from both ends of the market-structure spectrum: like perfect competition, it has many firms and free entry and exit, which drives economic profit to zero in the long run; but like monopoly, each firm has some pricing power because its product is not perfectly identical to its rivals' products, giving it a small downward-sloping demand curve rather than a perfectly flat one. For example, restaurants in a city compete under monopolistic competition, since each offers a somewhat different dining experience but faces many competing establishments offering similar, substitutable meals. The key long-run outcome that distinguishes it from perfect competition is excess capacity: because each firm's demand curve is still downward-sloping rather than flat, long-run equilibrium occurs where price equals average total cost, but not at the minimum point of that average-cost curve, leaving firms operating below their most efficient scale even though they earn zero economic profit. This trade-off, slightly higher costs in exchange for the genuine product variety consumers value, is central to evaluating whether monopolistic competition is "good" or "bad" for consumers. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 8: Monopolistic Competition](chapters/08-monopolistic-competition/index.md)

### How is market equilibrium determined, and what happens if price starts above or below it?

Market equilibrium is the price and quantity at which the quantity buyers wish to purchase exactly equals the quantity sellers wish to offer, found graphically where the demand curve and supply curve intersect; at that point, there is no economic pressure for price to change. If price starts above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating a market surplus (unsold goods piling up), which pressures sellers to cut prices to clear their inventory, moving price back down toward equilibrium. If price starts below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating a market shortage, which lets sellers raise prices without losing many sales, since eager buyers will pay more rather than go without, moving price back up toward equilibrium. For example, if a popular concert sets ticket prices too low, demand outstrips the available seats, creating a shortage that in an unregulated market would push the price upward until it clears. This self-correcting tendency is why markets tend to settle at equilibrium even without anyone deliberately aiming for it: it is simply the only price where neither buyers' nor sellers' behavior creates further pressure for change. Government price controls, like a price ceiling or floor, work by deliberately preventing the market from reaching this natural equilibrium. [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### How do I apply the laws of demand and supply to predict what happens to equilibrium price and quantity when demand or supply shifts?

The standard method is to shift one curve at a time, redraw the graph, and read the new intersection. If demand increases (the curve shifts right), both equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity rise, because at the old price there is now excess demand pulling price up, and the higher price draws out more quantity supplied along the existing supply curve. If demand decreases (shifts left), both equilibrium price and quantity fall. If supply increases (the curve shifts right), equilibrium price falls while equilibrium quantity rises, since more is being offered at every price, and buyers are drawn to buy more only once the price moves down. If supply decreases (shifts left), equilibrium price rises while equilibrium quantity falls. When both curves shift at the same time, you can determine the direction of the equilibrium quantity change if both shifts push it the same way, but the price change may be ambiguous without knowing the relative size of each shift, and vice versa for quantity. For example, a drought (supply decreases) combined with a health trend raising demand for a vegetable will unambiguously raise its equilibrium price, but the effect on quantity depends on which shift is larger. Practicing this one-curve-at-a-time method on many scenarios is the fastest way to build reliable intuition for supply-and-demand problems. [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What is price elasticity of demand, and what does a specific numerical value tell you about buyer behavior?

Price elasticity of demand measures how much the quantity demanded of a good changes in percentage terms in response to a one-percent change in its price; it is essentially a measure of how sensitive buyers are to price changes. A specific numerical value tells you the magnitude of that sensitivity: an elasticity of -2 means a 1% price increase causes quantity demanded to fall by about 2%, which economists call elastic demand (elasticity greater than 1 in absolute value), while an elasticity of -0.5 means the same 1% price increase causes only a 0.5% fall in quantity demanded, which is inelastic demand (less than 1 in absolute value). An elasticity of exactly -1 is unit-elastic. For example, demand for a specific brand of soda tends to be elastic because buyers can easily switch to another brand, while demand for insulin tends to be inelastic because patients who need it keep buying it even as price rises significantly. Knowing whether a good's demand is elastic or inelastic has immediate practical value: it tells a seller whether raising price will raise or lower total revenue, and it tells a policymaker who actually bears the burden of a tax placed on that good. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 3: Elasticity](chapters/03-elasticity/index.md)

### How do I use marginal analysis to decide how much of something to produce or consume?

The method is always the same: identify the marginal benefit and marginal cost of one additional unit, and keep going as long as marginal benefit is at least as large as marginal cost, stopping exactly where they are equal (or where the next unit would make marginal cost exceed marginal benefit). For a consumer, this might mean comparing the extra satisfaction (marginal utility) from one more slice of pizza to its price; for a firm, it means comparing the extra revenue from producing one more unit (marginal revenue) to the extra cost of producing it (marginal cost). The key discipline is to ignore totals and ignore sunk costs, focusing only on the next increment. For example, a firm should not ask "is this product line profitable overall?" when deciding whether to produce one more unit; it should ask "does the revenue from this specific next unit exceed the cost of making it?" Even a firm with large total losses should keep producing additional units as long as each one individually adds more revenue than cost, since refusing to produce a worthwhile marginal unit only makes the total loss larger. This marginal, unit-by-unit logic is the single most reused decision rule in the entire course. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics](chapters/01-introduction-to-microeconomics/index.md)

### How do I use the MR = MC rule to find a profit-maximizing firm's output level?

The MR = MC rule states that a firm maximizes profit by producing the quantity of output at which marginal revenue equals marginal cost; producing less than this quantity leaves profitable units unproduced, while producing more than this quantity means the last units cost more to make than they bring in. To apply it, write down or sketch the firm's marginal revenue curve and marginal cost curve as functions of quantity, then find the output level where they cross. For a price-taking competitive firm, marginal revenue is simply the constant market price, so the rule simplifies to producing where price equals marginal cost. For a firm with market power, like a monopolist, marginal revenue is below price and declining, so you must find marginal revenue specifically (not just use the price) before setting it equal to marginal cost. For example, if a firm's marginal cost of the 100th unit is $8 and its marginal revenue from that unit is $10, it should produce that unit, since it adds $2 to profit; but if the 101st unit's marginal cost rises to $11 while marginal revenue stays near $10, the firm should stop at 100 units. This same rule, with the details of marginal revenue changing by market structure, appears again in every market-structure chapter. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### What is the production possibilities frontier (PPF), and how do I use it to show opportunity cost and the gains from specialization?

The production possibilities frontier is a curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy (or a firm, or a person) can produce given its fixed resources and current technology; for example, a frontier might show an economy can produce 100 cars and 0 computers, 0 cars and 200 computers, or any combination of the two along the curve between them. Any point on the frontier represents full, efficient use of resources, any point inside it represents waste or unemployment of resources, and any point outside it is currently unattainable. You use the PPF to show opportunity cost by comparing two points on the curve: moving from one point to another and giving up some units of one good to gain units of the other reveals the opportunity cost of that trade-off directly, and the PPF is typically bowed outward because of increasing opportunity cost, since resources are not equally well suited to every use. You use it to show the gains from specialization and trade by comparing two separate producers' individual frontiers to a combined frontier: if each producer specializes in the good for which they have a comparative advantage (the lower opportunity cost) and then trades, the two together can consume combinations of goods that lie outside what either frontier alone could produce. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics](chapters/01-introduction-to-microeconomics/index.md)

### What is consumer optimum, and how does a consumer use marginal reasoning to decide what bundle of goods to buy?

Consumer optimum is the combination of goods a consumer chooses that maximizes total satisfaction (utility) given the limits of their budget constraint; for example, a consumer's optimum might be 4 apples and 3 oranges, the affordable bundle that leaves her as satisfied as possible among everything she could afford. The marginal reasoning behind finding it is the equal marginal principle: a consumer with a limited budget maximizes total utility by allocating spending so that the marginal utility per dollar spent is equal across every good purchased. If a dollar spent on apples currently buys more extra satisfaction than a dollar spent on oranges, the consumer is not yet at the optimum and should shift spending toward apples; she keeps reallocating until an additional dollar would yield the same extra satisfaction no matter which good it was spent on. For example, a consumer stops buying more apples and starts buying oranges once a dollar spent on oranges yields as much extra satisfaction as a dollar spent on apples. This same logic, formalized graphically using indifference curves and a budget line, is what Chapter 4 calls the tangency condition, where the consumer's optimum occurs where the budget line is just tangent to the highest attainable indifference curve. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 4: Consumer Theory](chapters/04-consumer-theory/index.md)

### How do economies of scale shape a firm's long-run average cost curve, and what does that mean for how big a firm should grow?

Economies of scale occur when a firm's long-run average cost falls as it increases the scale of its output, often because fixed costs like expensive machinery get spread over more units, or because larger scale allows specialization and bulk purchasing discounts. For example, a car manufacturer that spreads the cost of expensive machinery over a much larger number of vehicles experiences economies of scale, lowering its average cost per car as volume rises. Diseconomies of scale occur at the opposite extreme: long-run average cost rises as output grows further, often due to coordination and management difficulties within a very large organization, such as added layers of management slowing decisions and raising costs per unit. Together these two forces give the long-run average cost curve its typical U-shape, falling at first due to economies of scale and eventually rising due to diseconomies of scale, with the minimum point called the minimum efficient scale, the smallest output level at which the firm achieves its lowest possible average cost. For a firm deciding how big to grow, this curve indicates the output range where it is operating most cost-efficiently; growing well past the minimum efficient scale into the diseconomies-of-scale region raises average cost and can leave the firm less able to compete on price. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 5: Production and Costs](chapters/05-production-and-costs/index.md)

### Why does deadweight loss arise when a market produces something other than the efficient quantity, and how does this connect the efficiency of perfect competition to the inefficiency of monopoly?

Deadweight loss is the loss of total surplus to society that occurs when a market produces less (or more) than the efficient quantity, the quantity at which price equals marginal cost. It arises because, at any quantity below the efficient level, there exist potential trades between buyers and sellers, a buyer willing to pay more than it would cost the seller to produce, that simply never happen, destroying the surplus those trades would have created. In long-run competitive equilibrium under perfect competition, free entry and exit and price-taking behavior push price down to equal marginal cost exactly, so no such mutually beneficial trade is left unmade, and total surplus is maximized with zero deadweight loss, this is allocative efficiency. A monopolist, by contrast, deliberately restricts output below this efficient quantity in order to raise price and maximize its own profit, since it sets marginal revenue equal to marginal cost rather than price equal to marginal cost, and marginal revenue lies below price for a firm with market power. The resulting gap between the competitive outcome and the monopoly outcome, is exactly the monopoly's deadweight loss: the trades between willing buyers and the firm's low-cost production capacity that never happen because the monopolist found it more profitable to restrict output and charge more. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 7: Monopoly](chapters/07-monopoly/index.md)

### How are the different types of market failure, externalities, public goods, market power, and information problems, related to each other?

All of these market failures share a common root: in each case, the price a decision-maker faces fails to reflect the true social cost or benefit of their choice, so the market's otherwise reliable "invisible hand" steers resources to an inefficient outcome. With externalities, a third party bears a cost or enjoys a benefit that never shows up in the price the buyer and seller negotiate, so the private market price understates the true social cost (negative externalities) or benefit (positive externalities) of the activity. With public goods, the problem is that non-excludability lets people free-ride, consuming the good without paying for it, so no individual seller can charge a price that reflects the good's full social value, leading to underprovision relative to what society would actually want. With market power, as in monopoly, the firm deliberately restricts output to raise price above marginal cost, meaning the price again fails to reflect the true marginal cost of production. With information problems like adverse selection and moral hazard, one side of a transaction cannot accurately observe quality or behavior, so the price charged reflects an average that misprices individual, differently-informed participants. In every case, the common fix is a policy tool, taxes, subsidies, regulation, or better information design, that realigns the price signal with the true social cost or benefit. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 12: Market Failures](chapters/12-market-failures/index.md)

### What is oligopoly, and why does strategic interdependence make it harder to analyze than perfect competition or monopoly?

Oligopoly is a market structure dominated by a small number of large firms whose decisions about price and output significantly affect one another; the commercial airplane manufacturing industry, dominated by just a couple of major producers, is a real-world example. What makes oligopoly fundamentally different, and harder to analyze, from perfect competition and monopoly is strategic interdependence: the condition in which each firm's best choice of price or output depends directly on the choices made by its rivals. A perfectly competitive firm can ignore its competitors entirely, since no single firm's output decision moves the market price; a monopolist faces no rivals at all, only the overall market demand curve. An oligopolist, by contrast, must explicitly predict and react to what its rivals will do, since a price cut or output increase by one firm changes the profitable choice for every other firm in the market. This is precisely why oligopoly cannot be solved with the simple price-taking or single-firm-demand tools used in earlier chapters, and why the course introduces formal game theory, dominant strategies, Nash equilibrium, and backward induction immediately after the oligopoly chapter, as the dedicated toolkit for reasoning through this kind of mutual, interdependent decision-making. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 9: Oligopoly](chapters/09-oligopoly/index.md)

## Technical Detail Questions

### What is the formal definition of opportunity cost, and how is it different from a sunk cost?

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative given up when a person or society chooses one option over another; it is always forward-looking, describing what you are giving up by making a choice right now. A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered, and it is always backward-looking, describing money or effort already spent in the past. The crucial technical distinction is that rational decision-making should always account fully for opportunity costs but should always ignore sunk costs, because a sunk cost does not change regardless of what you decide to do next, while an opportunity cost does change depending on what alternative you choose. For example, money already spent on a non-refundable concert ticket is a sunk cost and should not influence whether you attend if a better opportunity arises, since that money is gone either way; but the opportunity cost of attending the concert anyway, namely the value of whatever else you could have done with that evening, is very much relevant to the decision. Students often conflate the two, reasoning "I already paid for this, so I should use it," which is exactly the sunk cost fallacy this course warns against from Chapter 1 onward. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics](chapters/01-introduction-to-microeconomics/index.md)

### What is the difference between a normal good and an inferior good?

A normal good is a good for which quantity demanded rises when buyer income rises, and falls when income falls, holding price constant; restaurant dining is a normal good for most people, since they eat out more often as their income increases. An inferior good is the opposite: quantity demanded falls when income rises, and rises when income falls, holding price constant; instant noodles may be an inferior good if people buy less of them once they can afford pricier meals as their income grows. The label "inferior" describes the good's relationship to income, not its physical quality; an inferior good can be perfectly fine, it is simply something people buy less of as they become able to afford better alternatives. This classification is formalized by income elasticity of demand: a positive income elasticity identifies a normal good, while a negative income elasticity identifies an inferior good. Both classifications are determined empirically by observing how quantity demanded actually responds to income changes for a specific good and a specific population, which means the same good could in principle be a normal good for one group of buyers and an inferior good for another, depending on their circumstances and preferences. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What are substitute goods and complement goods, and how can you tell them apart?

Substitute goods are two goods for which an increase in the price of one causes an increase in demand for the other, because buyers can use either one to satisfy a similar need; butter and margarine are substitute goods, since a higher price of butter tends to increase demand for margarine as buyers switch toward it. Complement goods are two goods for which an increase in the price of one causes a decrease in demand for the other, because the two goods are typically used together; coffee and coffee creamer are complements, so a higher price of coffee tends to decrease demand for creamer, since people buy less coffee overall. The technical way to tell them apart is the sign of their cross-price elasticity of demand: a positive cross-price elasticity (quantity demanded of good A rises when the price of good B rises) identifies substitutes, while a negative cross-price elasticity identifies complements. In practice, you can usually tell which relationship applies to two particular goods by asking whether they are typically used as alternatives to each other (substitutes) or used together in the same activity (complements), though the cross-price elasticity calculation gives the precise, data-based answer when the intuitive classification is ambiguous. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What is the difference between fixed cost and variable cost?

Fixed cost is a cost of production that does not change with the quantity of output produced in the short run; a factory's rent payment is a fixed cost because it must be paid whether the factory produces 100 units or zero units that month. Variable cost is a cost of production that does change with the quantity of output produced; the cost of raw materials used in manufacturing rises and falls with the number of units a factory produces, making it a variable cost. The distinction is specifically a short-run concept: in the long run, a firm can adjust every input, including ones that were fixed in the short run (like plant size), which is why economists say there are no truly fixed costs in the long run. This distinction matters directly for a firm's shutdown decision: because fixed costs must be paid regardless of output level, a firm comparing whether to produce at all in the short run should ignore fixed costs and compare price only to average variable cost, since fixed costs are unavoidable either way and therefore irrelevant at the margin, in the same spirit as ignoring a sunk cost. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 5: Production and Costs](chapters/05-production-and-costs/index.md)

### What is a price taker versus a price maker?

A price taker is a buyer or seller in a market who has no ability to influence the market price and must simply accept the price determined by the overall market; a wheat farmer selling into a large competitive market is a price taker, since his individual decision to sell more or less has no noticeable effect on the going market price. A price maker is a seller with enough market power to influence the price of its product by changing the quantity it offers for sale, rather than simply accepting a price handed to it by the market; a monopolist is the clearest example, since it is the only seller and therefore directly determines the trade-off between quantity sold and the price charged. The technical difference shows up in each firm's marginal revenue: a price taker's marginal revenue equals the fixed market price for every unit sold, giving it a perfectly flat (horizontal) demand curve from its own point of view, while a price maker's marginal revenue lies below price and falls as output rises, because selling one more unit requires lowering the price on every unit sold, not just the additional one. Whether a firm is a price taker or price maker is the single most important fact determining which market-structure model (perfect competition versus monopoly, monopolistic competition, or oligopoly) applies to it. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### What are the four classic market structures, and what feature mainly distinguishes them?

The four classic market structures covered in this course are perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, and oligopoly. The feature that mainly distinguishes them is the number of sellers combined with how differentiated their products are and how much individual pricing power each firm has as a result. Perfect competition has many sellers with an identical product and no pricing power (pure price takers). Monopoly has a single seller with no close substitutes and full pricing power (a pure price maker). Monopolistic competition has many sellers like perfect competition, but each sells a somewhat differentiated product, giving each firm a small amount of pricing power. Oligopoly has only a small number of large sellers, who may sell identical or differentiated products, but whose decisions are strategically interdependent, meaning each firm's choices directly affect, and are affected by, its rivals' choices in a way that does not occur in the other three structures. Economists also use measures like the concentration ratio and the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index to classify real industries into one of these four categories based on how concentrated market share is among the top firms. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### What's the difference between economic profit and accounting profit?

Accounting profit is the difference between a firm's total revenue and its explicit costs only, meaning costs that involve an actual cash payment, such as wages, rent, and materials; it is the profit figure that would appear on a conventional accounting statement. Economic profit is the difference between total revenue and total costs, including both those same explicit costs and the implicit opportunity cost of resources the owner supplies themselves, such as the wages they gave up by not working elsewhere or the return they gave up by investing their own capital in this business instead of another one. For example, a business owner earning $50,000 in accounting profit but who gave up a $60,000 salaried job to run the business actually has an economic loss of $10,000, even though the accounting books show a positive profit. This difference matters because economists use economic profit, not accounting profit, to determine whether a firm should actually stay in an industry: zero economic profit means the firm is earning exactly enough to cover every cost, including opportunity costs, which is precisely the condition that defines long-run competitive equilibrium, even though the firm's accounting profit in that state is typically positive. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### What's the difference between marginal cost and average total cost, and why can one be rising while the other is falling?

Marginal cost is the additional cost incurred from producing one more unit of output; average total cost is total cost divided by the quantity of output produced, essentially the cost per unit averaged across everything made so far. They can move in opposite directions at the same output level because of a basic mathematical relationship: whenever marginal cost is below average total cost, producing one more unit pulls the average down, so average total cost is falling; whenever marginal cost is above average total cost, the next unit pulls the average up, so average total cost is rising. This is exactly why the marginal cost curve crosses the average total cost curve precisely at the average total cost curve's minimum point: marginal cost is below average cost (pulling it down) up to that point, and above average cost (pulling it up) after it. For example, if a firm's average total cost is $10 per unit but the next unit costs only $7 to produce, making that unit lowers the average to somewhere between $7 and $10, even though the firm's total cost has risen. Understanding this relationship is essential for correctly reading any cost-curve diagram in Chapters 5 and 6. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 5: Production and Costs](chapters/05-production-and-costs/index.md)

### What's the difference between allocative efficiency and productive efficiency?

Productive efficiency is a situation in which goods are produced using the fewest possible resources, so no more of one good can be produced without producing less of another; it corresponds to operating exactly on the production possibilities frontier rather than inside it, and for a firm it typically means producing at the minimum point of its average total cost curve. Allocative efficiency is a stronger, different condition: resources are allocated so that producing one more unit of a good provides a benefit to society exactly equal to its cost, meaning price equals marginal cost. A market can be productively efficient (each firm minimizes its own cost per unit) without being allocatively efficient (the market as a whole still produces too little or too much relative to what society actually values). Perfectly competitive markets achieve both in long-run equilibrium, since free entry and exit drives firms to their minimum average cost (productive efficiency) at the same quantity where price equals marginal cost (allocative efficiency). A monopoly, by contrast, typically fails to achieve allocative efficiency, because it deliberately restricts output and prices above marginal cost, even if the firm itself is internally well-managed and minimizes its own production costs. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### What's the difference between a change in demand and a change in quantity demanded?

A change in quantity demanded refers only to a movement along a fixed, unchanged demand curve, caused exclusively by a change in the good's own price; nothing about the underlying relationship between price and buyer behavior has changed, only where on that existing relationship you currently are. A change in demand refers to the entire demand curve shifting to a new position on the graph, caused by a change in one of the determinants of demand other than the good's own price, such as income, tastes, the price of a related good, buyer expectations, or the number of buyers. For example, a rise in consumer income is a determinant of demand that can shift the entire demand curve for restaurant meals rightward (a change in demand), whereas a restaurant simply lowering its own prices and selling more meals as a result is only a movement along its existing demand curve (a change in quantity demanded). This exact distinction also applies on the supply side, between a change in supply (the curve shifts) and a change in quantity supplied (movement along a fixed curve caused only by the good's own price). Mixing these up is one of the most common mistakes on supply-and-demand exam problems. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### What's the difference between the income effect and the substitution effect?

The substitution effect is the change in quantity demanded of a good that results from a change in its price relative to other goods, holding the consumer's overall satisfaction level constant; when the price of beef rises relative to chicken, consumers who buy more chicken instead of beef are responding to the substitution effect, since beef has become relatively more expensive regardless of their income. The income effect is the change in quantity demanded that results from a change in a consumer's purchasing power caused by the price change itself, holding relative prices constant; when the price of gasoline falls, a driver's effective purchasing power rises, and the resulting increase in driving distance partly reflects the income effect, separate from any substitution toward driving relative to other activities. Both effects normally push a demand curve in the same direction for a normal good, which is exactly why ordinary demand curves slope downward so reliably. The two effects can work against each other for an inferior good, and in the rare theoretical case of a Giffen good, a very strong negative income effect for an essential inferior good can actually outweigh the substitution effect, causing quantity demanded to fall as price falls, violating the usual law of demand. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 4: Consumer Theory](chapters/04-consumer-theory/index.md)

### What's the difference between adverse selection and moral hazard?

Adverse selection is a problem arising from information asymmetry in which the party with less information ends up disproportionately attracting the riskiest or lowest-quality counterparts before a transaction occurs; an insurance company offering the same health insurance premium to everyone may attract mostly sicker applicants, since healthier people find the premium less worthwhile. Moral hazard is a different problem, also arising from information asymmetry, but it occurs after a transaction takes place: one party changes their behavior to take on more risk because the other party bears some of the consequences of that risk. A driver with full car insurance coverage might drive a little less carefully, knowing the insurance company will cover most of the cost of any accident, illustrating moral hazard. The key technical distinction is timing and mechanism: adverse selection is about who selects into a transaction based on hidden characteristics known before the deal is struck, while moral hazard is about how behavior changes after the deal is struck because incentives to be careful have been weakened. Both problems commonly coexist in the same market, such as health insurance, which is part of why insurance contracts often include deductibles and co-payments, designed to address moral hazard, alongside medical underwriting, designed to address adverse selection. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 14: Information Economics](chapters/14-information-economics/index.md)

### What's the difference between a negative externality and a positive externality?

A negative externality is an externality in which a third party bears a cost from an economic activity, causing the market to produce more of the good than is socially efficient, because the price charged does not reflect the full social cost; air pollution from a power plant that harms the health of nearby residents who have no say in the plant's operation is a negative externality. A positive externality is the opposite: a third party receives a benefit from an economic activity, causing the market to produce less of the good than is socially efficient, because the price charged does not reflect the full social benefit; a beekeeper's bees pollinating a neighboring farmer's crops, boosting that farmer's harvest for free, is a positive externality. The policy implication is also opposite: a negative externality calls for a Pigouvian tax, set equal to the external cost per unit, to discourage overproduction, while a positive externality calls for a Pigouvian subsidy, set equal to the external benefit per unit, to encourage more of the socially beneficial activity. In both cases, the goal is the same: make the private decision-maker's incentives reflect the full social marginal cost or benefit of the activity, not just their own private cost or benefit. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 13: Externalities and Public Goods](chapters/13-externalities-and-public-goods/index.md)

### What's the difference between a public good and a common-pool resource?

Both are classified using two properties: excludability, whether it is possible to prevent non-payers from consuming a good, and rivalry, whether one person's consumption reduces what is available to others. A public good is both non-excludable and non-rival, meaning it is difficult to prevent people from using it without paying, and one person's use does not reduce its availability to others; national defense is a public good because protecting the country does not prevent any individual citizen from also being protected, and non-payers cannot easily be excluded. A common-pool resource is non-excludable but rival, meaning it is still difficult to prevent access, but one person's use does reduce what remains for others; fish in an open ocean are a common-pool resource, since anyone can fish there, but each fish caught is no longer available to other fishers. Both share the free-riding problem that comes from non-excludability, but only the common-pool resource also suffers from the tragedy of the commons, overuse and depletion because individual users bear only part of the cost of their own use, which is why fisheries and shared pastures face a distinct overuse problem that national defense does not. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 13: Externalities and Public Goods](chapters/13-externalities-and-public-goods/index.md)

### How do you calculate average total cost, and what does it mean if it's higher than price?

Average total cost is calculated by dividing total cost (the sum of fixed cost and variable cost) by the quantity of output produced; equivalently, it equals average fixed cost plus average variable cost. For example, with a fixed cost of $1,000 and a variable cost of $500 for 100 units, total cost is $1,500, so average total cost is $15 per unit. If the market price is below average total cost, the firm is losing money on every unit sold on average, since each unit's cost per unit exceeds the revenue per unit it earns. This does not necessarily mean the firm should shut down immediately, though: in the short run, the relevant comparison for the shutdown decision is price versus average variable cost, not average total cost, because fixed costs must be paid whether the firm produces or not. A firm with price below average total cost but above average variable cost should keep operating in the short run, since it is at least covering its variable costs and recovering part of its unavoidable fixed costs, even though it is suffering an economic loss overall; in the long run, however, persistent price below average total cost signals that firms should exit the industry. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 5: Production and Costs](chapters/05-production-and-costs/index.md)

### How do you use the shutdown rule to decide whether a competitive firm should keep producing in the short run?

The shutdown rule states that a firm should stop producing in the short run if the price it receives is less than its average variable cost, since it would lose less money by shutting down (losing only its unavoidable fixed costs) than by operating (losing fixed costs plus the gap between price and variable cost on every unit). To apply it, compare the current market price to the firm's average variable cost at the output level where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. If price is above average variable cost, even if it is below average total cost, the firm should keep producing, because it is covering all of its variable costs and recovering at least part of its fixed costs, which is better than covering none of them by shutting down. If price falls below average variable cost, the firm should shut down temporarily, since continuing to operate would mean losing money on every single unit produced, beyond just the unavoidable fixed costs. For example, a firm facing a price below its average variable cost shuts down rather than operate, because continuing to produce would lose even more than its unavoidable fixed costs alone. This rule only applies in the short run; in the long run, a firm comparing price to average total cost should exit the industry entirely if it cannot cover all its costs. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### How do you tell whether demand is elastic, inelastic, or unit-elastic from the price elasticity number?

Take the absolute value of the calculated price elasticity of demand and compare it to 1. If the absolute value is greater than 1, demand is elastic, meaning quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than the price change that caused it. If the absolute value is less than 1, demand is inelastic, meaning quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than the price change. If the absolute value is exactly 1, demand is unit-elastic, meaning the percentage change in quantity demanded exactly equals the percentage change in price. Two further special cases sit at the extremes: perfectly elastic demand, represented by a horizontal demand curve, where buyers will purchase any amount at one specific price but none at all at any higher price; and perfectly inelastic demand, represented by a vertical demand curve, where quantity demanded does not change at all regardless of the price charged. A useful shortcut that avoids calculating elasticity directly is the total revenue test: if raising price causes total revenue to fall, demand is elastic over that range; if raising price causes total revenue to rise, demand is inelastic over that range; and if total revenue stays the same, demand is unit-elastic. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 3: Elasticity](chapters/03-elasticity/index.md)

### How do you calculate producer surplus and consumer surplus from a market diagram?

On a standard supply-and-demand diagram with the equilibrium price and quantity marked, consumer surplus is the area of the triangle bounded above by the demand curve, below by the horizontal line at the equilibrium price, and on the right by the vertical line at the equilibrium quantity; it represents the total extra value buyers received beyond what they actually paid, summed across every unit sold. Producer surplus is the mirror-image triangle: bounded below by the supply curve, above by the horizontal line at the equilibrium price, and on the right by the vertical line at the equilibrium quantity; it represents the total extra revenue sellers received beyond the minimum they would have accepted, summed across every unit sold. For simple linear demand and supply curves, each triangle's area can be calculated using the standard triangle-area formula, one-half times base times height, where the base is the equilibrium quantity and the height is the vertical distance between the equilibrium price and where the relevant curve meets the vertical price axis (for consumer surplus) or the quantity axis origin price (for producer surplus). Adding the two triangles together gives total surplus, the standard measure of the overall gains from trade a market generates at equilibrium. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 2: Supply and Demand](chapters/02-supply-and-demand/index.md)

### How do economies of scale and diseconomies of scale together shape the long-run average cost curve's U-shape?

At low levels of output, a firm typically experiences economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as output rises, because fixed investments like specialized machinery get spread over more units, and larger scale often allows bulk input discounts and worker specialization. This creates the initial downward-sloping portion of the U-shaped long-run average cost curve. As output keeps growing, the firm eventually reaches its minimum efficient scale, the output level where long-run average cost reaches its lowest point. Beyond that point, many firms experience diseconomies of scale: long-run average cost starts rising again as output grows further, typically due to coordination and management difficulties that come with running a very large organization, such as added layers of management slowing decision-making and raising costs per unit. The combination of falling average cost at low output (economies of scale) and rising average cost at very high output (diseconomies of scale) is exactly what produces the characteristic U-shape, and the width of the flat bottom of that U, how large a range of output achieves close to the minimum cost, helps explain real-world industry structure: industries with a very wide efficient range, like utilities, tend toward natural monopoly, while industries with a narrow efficient range tend to support many competing firms. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 5: Production and Costs](chapters/05-production-and-costs/index.md)

### What's the difference between first-degree, second-degree, and third-degree price discrimination, and how does the information each requires differ?

First-degree price discrimination charges each individual buyer the maximum price that specific buyer is willing to pay, capturing all consumer surplus as profit; a car salesperson who negotiates a separate, maximum price with each customer individually is attempting first-degree discrimination, and it requires the seller to know, or successfully negotiate out, each buyer's individual willingness to pay, which is informationally demanding and rarely achieved perfectly in practice. Second-degree price discrimination offers different prices or terms based on the quantity purchased or the version of the product chosen, letting buyers sort themselves into price categories; a phone company offering cheaper per-gigabyte rates on larger data plans is practicing second-degree discrimination, and it requires much less information, since the seller designs a menu of options and buyers self-select based on their own usage patterns, without the seller needing to know each buyer individually. Third-degree price discrimination charges different prices to distinct groups of customers identified by an observable characteristic, such as age or location; airlines charging different fares for business travelers versus leisure travelers who book well in advance are practicing third-degree discrimination, requiring only that the seller can identify group membership, such as a student ID, not individual willingness to pay. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 7: Monopoly](chapters/07-monopoly/index.md)

## Common Challenges

### What is the midpoint elasticity formula, and why do economists use it instead of simple percentage changes?

The midpoint elasticity formula calculates price elasticity of demand by dividing the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in price, but it computes each percentage change by dividing the raw change by the average (midpoint) of the initial and final values, rather than by only the initial value. Economists use this version specifically because a simple percentage change calculated from the initial value gives a different, inconsistent elasticity depending on whether price is rising or falling between the same two points; for example, going from $10 to $8 is a 20% decrease using $10 as the base, but going from $8 back to $10 is a 25% increase using $8 as the base, even though it is the exact same two points on the demand curve. The midpoint formula fixes this inconsistency by using the same average value as the denominator in both directions, so the calculated elasticity between any two points is identical whether you are moving from the first point to the second or from the second back to the first. This matters in practice whenever a problem does not clearly tell you which price is the "starting" price, which is common, since the midpoint formula removes the ambiguity about which direction you are supposedly moving. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 3: Elasticity](chapters/03-elasticity/index.md)

### Why do students often confuse "elastic" with "big demand," and what does elasticity actually measure?

A very common misconception is thinking that "elastic demand" means a good is in high demand or popular, or that "inelastic" means a good is unpopular. In fact, elasticity has nothing to do with how much of a good is purchased in absolute terms; it measures only how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in price, that is, the ratio of a percentage change in quantity to a percentage change in price. A good can have enormous total demand, meaning huge quantities are purchased, while still having highly elastic demand if buyers would sharply cut back purchases in response to even a small price increase. For example, demand for a specific brand of soda can be very high in absolute terms, with millions of cans sold, while still being elastic, since buyers can easily switch to another brand the moment its price rises noticeably. Conversely, demand for insulin is inelastic, meaning quantity barely changes with price, even though total quantity purchased might be modest relative to a mass-market product. The accurate mental model is: elasticity describes the shape and steepness of the responsiveness relationship, not the size or popularity of the market, and the two concepts are measured completely independently of one another. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 3: Elasticity](chapters/03-elasticity/index.md)

### What is the tangency condition in consumer theory, and what does it mean geometrically?

The tangency condition is the requirement that, at the consumer optimum, the budget line just touches the highest attainable indifference curve without crossing it, so the marginal rate of substitution equals the ratio of the two goods' prices. Geometrically, this means the slope of the indifference curve (the marginal rate of substitution, the rate at which the consumer is willing to trade one good for the other while staying equally satisfied) exactly equals the slope of the budget line (the negative of the ratio of the two goods' prices) at the chosen bundle. If the two slopes are not equal at a particular point on the budget line, the consumer is not yet at the optimum: she could trade along the budget line, giving up some of one good for more of the other, and reach a higher indifference curve while still staying within her budget. Students often find this condition hard because it combines two separate curves (an indifference curve representing preferences and a straight budget line representing what is affordable) and asks for the one point where their slopes match exactly, rather than, say, where they intersect at all (which happens at many points) or where total utility is highest in isolation. Practicing by sketching several indifference curves crossing a single budget line, and visually identifying the one tangent point, builds the geometric intuition faster than algebra alone. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 4: Consumer Theory](chapters/04-consumer-theory/index.md)

### What is a Nash equilibrium, and why isn't it necessarily the best outcome for the players?

A Nash equilibrium is a situation in a game in which every player is choosing the best strategy available given the strategies chosen by all other players, so no single player can benefit by changing their own strategy alone, holding everyone else's strategy fixed. The key word is "alone": a Nash equilibrium only requires that no individual player regrets their choice unilaterally, not that the outcome is good for the players collectively, or even that any player is actually happy with the result. This is exactly what makes the prisoner's dilemma so unsettling: both players confessing is a Nash equilibrium, since neither player can improve their own outcome by switching strategies while the other stays put, yet both players would have been strictly better off if they had both stayed silent instead. The reason staying silent together is not a Nash equilibrium is that each player, individually, could improve their own payoff by confessing while the other stays silent, so the "both cooperate" outcome is unstable even though it is mutually preferable. Students sometimes mistakenly treat Nash equilibrium as a synonym for "the best possible outcome," but it is better understood as "the self-consistent outcome once everyone is behaving in their own rational self-interest," which can diverge sharply from the group-optimal outcome. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 10: Game Theory](chapters/10-game-theory/index.md)

### Why does a Pigouvian tax fix a negative externality instead of just banning the activity?

A Pigouvian tax is a tax levied on an activity that generates a negative externality, set equal to the external cost per unit, in order to align private incentives with the full social cost of the activity. The goal of correcting a negative externality is not to eliminate the activity entirely, but to reduce it to the socially efficient quantity, the level where the social marginal benefit of the activity equals its social marginal cost, including the external cost. A complete ban would eliminate not just the inefficient, overproduced units but also every unit of the activity that is still genuinely worth doing even after accounting for its external cost, destroying real value in the process. A Pigouvian tax, by contrast, raises the private cost of the activity by exactly the size of the external cost, so each producer or consumer now faces the true social cost of their decision and will naturally cut back only on the units whose benefit no longer exceeds the full social cost, while continuing units that are still worthwhile overall. For example, a tax on each ton of carbon emitted is a Pigouvian tax designed to make polluters pay for the climate damage their emissions cause to others, discouraging excessive pollution while still allowing economically valuable production to continue at a more efficient, lower level. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 13: Externalities and Public Goods](chapters/13-externalities-and-public-goods/index.md)

### How do you calculate price elasticity of demand using the midpoint formula, step by step?

First, identify the initial price and quantity (P1, Q1) and the new price and quantity (P2, Q2) from the problem. Second, calculate the percentage change in quantity using the midpoint formula: divide the change in quantity (Q2 − Q1) by the average of the two quantities ((Q1 + Q2) divided by 2), then multiply by 100. Third, calculate the percentage change in price the same way: divide the change in price (P2 − P1) by the average of the two prices ((P1 + P2) divided by 2), then multiply by 100. Fourth, divide the percentage change in quantity by the percentage change in price to get the elasticity value. For example, if price rises from $8 to $12 and quantity demanded falls from 100 to 60 units, the percentage change in quantity is (60 − 100) divided by 80, equal to −50%, and the percentage change in price is (12 − 8) divided by 10, equal to 40%. Dividing −50% by 40% gives an elasticity of −1.25, which in absolute value is greater than 1, so demand is elastic over this price range. A common mistake is forgetting to use the average (midpoint) values in the denominators and instead using only the initial values, which produces an inconsistent elasticity depending on the direction of the price change. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 3: Elasticity](chapters/03-elasticity/index.md)

### How do you find the consumer optimum using the tangency condition between the budget line and an indifference curve?

Start by drawing the consumer's budget line, a straight line whose slope is the negative of the ratio of the two goods' prices and whose endpoints are determined by the consumer's income divided by each good's price. Then sketch a family of indifference curves, each representing a constant level of satisfaction, with curves farther from the origin representing higher satisfaction. The consumer optimum is the single point where one of these indifference curves is tangent to the budget line, touching it at exactly one point without crossing it, since this is the highest indifference curve the consumer can reach while staying within budget. Algebraically, you find this point by setting the marginal rate of substitution (the slope of the indifference curve) equal to the price ratio (the slope of the budget line), and solving that equation together with the budget constraint equation simultaneously, since you have two unknowns, the quantity of each good, and need two equations to solve for them. For example, if a consumer's marginal rate of substitution equals the ratio of apple price to orange price at a particular bundle, and that bundle also satisfies the budget constraint exactly, that bundle is the consumer optimum. Any point where the two slopes are not yet equal is not optimal, since the consumer could still trade along the budget line to reach a higher indifference curve. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 4: Consumer Theory](chapters/04-consumer-theory/index.md)

### How do you find the Nash equilibrium in a payoff matrix?

Use the "best response" method. First, for each strategy the column player might choose, identify the row player's best response, the strategy that gives the row player the highest payoff given that specific column choice, and mark it. Second, for each strategy the row player might choose, identify the column player's best response the same way, and mark it. Third, look for any cell in the matrix where both players' strategies are marked as a best response to each other simultaneously; that cell is a Nash equilibrium, since neither player would want to deviate unilaterally. A payoff matrix can have exactly one Nash equilibrium, multiple Nash equilibria (common in coordination games), or, in simultaneous games without repetition, sometimes no equilibrium in pure strategies at all. For example, in a classic prisoner's dilemma payoff matrix, checking all four cells reveals that "both confess" is the only cell where each player's strategy is a best response to the other's, making it the unique Nash equilibrium, even though "both stay silent" would give both players a better outcome. A useful shortcut: if a player has a dominant strategy, a strategy that is their best response no matter what the other player does, you can immediately mark every cell in that player's row or column consistent with playing it, which quickly narrows down where the equilibrium must be. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 10: Game Theory](chapters/10-game-theory/index.md)

### How do you solve a sequential game using backward induction?

Backward induction solves a sequential game by analyzing the very last decision in the game first, determining what the player making that final move would rationally do given everything that has already happened, and then working backward one step at a time through earlier decisions, each time assuming later players will behave exactly as already determined. Concretely, draw the game as a decision tree with branches representing each player's possible choices at each stage, and payoffs listed at the end of every branch. Start at the final decision nodes, nearest the payoffs, and circle whichever choice gives the player at that node the higher payoff, eliminating the other branch. Move up one level to the previous decision node, and now, knowing exactly what will happen at every later node because you just solved it, determine the best choice at this earlier node given those known later outcomes. Repeat until you reach the very first decision at the top of the tree. For example, using backward induction, an entrant predicts how an incumbent firm would actually respond to entry (by solving the incumbent's decision first), and then makes its own entry decision based on that predicted response, rather than being swayed by a threat the incumbent would not actually find profitable to carry out, which the course calls a non-credible threat. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 10: Game Theory](chapters/10-game-theory/index.md)

### How do you calculate the size of a deadweight loss triangle on a supply-and-demand diagram?

Deadweight loss appears on a diagram as a triangle sitting between the demand curve and the supply curve, bounded on one side by the efficient (competitive equilibrium) quantity and on the other side by the actual, distorted quantity that results from a tax, price control, or monopoly restriction. To calculate its area, use the standard triangle-area formula: one-half times the base times the height. The base of the triangle is the horizontal distance between the efficient quantity and the actual quantity, that is, how many units of trade were lost. The height of the triangle is the vertical gap at the actual quantity between the demand curve (what buyers would be willing to pay for that marginal unit) and the supply curve (what it would cost to supply that marginal unit), which represents the per-unit surplus that each lost unit would have generated had it been traded. For example, if a tax reduces quantity traded from 100 units to 80 units, and at 80 units buyers would pay $12 while sellers would need $8 to supply it, the deadweight loss triangle has a base of 20 units and a height of $4, giving an area of one-half times 20 times 4, equal to $40 of lost total surplus. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 7: Monopoly](chapters/07-monopoly/index.md)

### Why does the prisoner's dilemma produce a worse outcome for both players even though each is behaving rationally?

The prisoner's dilemma is a game in which each player has a dominant strategy that leads to a worse outcome for both players collectively than if they had cooperated instead, illustrating a direct conflict between individual rationality and group interest. Each player, reasoning entirely rationally and selfishly, compares their own payoff from each possible strategy against every possible choice the other player might make, and finds that one strategy, call it defecting or confessing, always gives them a strictly higher personal payoff no matter what the other player does. Because this logic applies symmetrically to both players, both rationally choose to defect, landing on the Nash equilibrium. The tragedy is that if both players had instead chosen to cooperate, both would have received a higher payoff than they get at the Nash equilibrium, but neither player can unilaterally choose that better outcome, because whichever one tries to cooperate while the other defects ends up with the worst individual payoff of all. For example, two firms that would both profit more by keeping prices high end up competing them down because each individually gains by undercutting the other, even though both would prefer the high-price outcome if they could somehow trust each other to stick with it. This tension is exactly why real-world oligopolies attempt collusion, and why repeated interaction and strategies like tit-for-tat can sometimes sustain cooperation that a single, one-shot game cannot. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 10: Game Theory](chapters/10-game-theory/index.md)

### How does information asymmetry explain why insurance markets can suffer from both adverse selection and moral hazard at the same time?

Information asymmetry means one party to a transaction has more or better information than the other, and insurance markets involve two distinct information gaps that create two distinct problems simultaneously. Before a policy is purchased, applicants know their own health, driving habits, or risk level far better than the insurer does, which creates adverse selection: at any given premium, people who already know they are higher risk are more likely to find that premium worthwhile and buy coverage, while lower-risk people are more likely to decline, skewing the pool of buyers toward higher average risk than the insurer priced for. After a policy is purchased, the insurer can no longer closely observe the policyholder's ongoing behavior, which creates moral hazard: knowing that the insurer will cover most losses, the insured person has a weaker incentive to take costly precautions, such as driving carefully or managing their health, than they would without coverage. These two problems compound each other in a way that is genuinely difficult to grasp at first, because they operate at different stages of the same relationship and call for different remedies: adverse selection is addressed by better screening and underwriting before a policy is issued, while moral hazard is addressed by deductibles, co-payments, and monitoring that restore some incentive for caution after coverage begins. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 14: Information Economics](chapters/14-information-economics/index.md)

## Best Practice Questions

### How should I decide which economic tool, marginal analysis, elasticity, or game theory, fits a given real-world decision?

Start by asking whether the decision involves only your own trade-off between an action's benefit and cost, with no other strategic actor reacting to your choice; if so, marginal analysis, comparing marginal benefit to marginal cost, is usually the right starting tool, whether you are a consumer deciding how much to buy or a firm deciding how much to produce. If the decision instead centers on how buyers or sellers will respond to a price change, specifically how much quantity will shift in percentage terms, elasticity is the right tool, since it directly measures that responsiveness and tells you things marginal analysis alone cannot, such as whether raising price will raise or lower total revenue. If the decision instead depends critically on what a rival, competitor, or counterpart will do in response to your own choice, and their best choice similarly depends on yours, you are in a strategic setting and need game theory, dominant strategies, Nash equilibrium, or backward induction for sequential moves, rather than marginal analysis or elasticity alone. In practice, many real decisions require combining tools: a firm might use elasticity to estimate how a price change affects its own demand, and then use game theory to predict whether a rival will match that price change, before applying marginal analysis to find the resulting profit-maximizing choice. [Chapter 1: Introduction to Microeconomics](chapters/01-introduction-to-microeconomics/index.md)

### How can a business use marginal analysis to decide whether to hire one more worker?

A business should hire an additional worker only if that worker's marginal revenue product, the additional revenue generated by their added output, is at least as large as the wage the firm must pay them; this is exactly the labor hiring rule covered in the factor markets chapter. To apply it in practice, estimate how much extra output the next worker would add (their marginal product) and multiply that by the extra revenue each additional unit of output brings in (marginal revenue), giving the marginal revenue product. Compare that figure to the wage rate the firm must pay to attract and retain that worker. If marginal revenue product exceeds the wage, hiring adds to profit and the firm should hire; if it falls below the wage, hiring that worker would reduce profit, and the firm should stop hiring at the current staffing level. For example, a firm should stop hiring once the next worker's marginal revenue product would be only $12 but the prevailing wage rate is $15, since that hire would actually reduce profit rather than increase it, even though the worker would still add some positive output. This same marginal comparison, extended across every additional worker, traces out the firm's entire labor demand curve. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 11: Factor Markets](chapters/11-factor-markets/index.md)

### How can a manager use price elasticity of demand to decide whether to raise or lower prices?

The manager should first estimate (or look up, if available) the price elasticity of demand for their specific product over the relevant price range. If demand is inelastic, meaning the elasticity's absolute value is less than 1, raising price will increase total revenue, since the percentage increase in price outweighs the smaller percentage decrease in quantity sold; conversely, lowering price would decrease total revenue in this case. If demand is elastic, meaning the elasticity's absolute value is greater than 1, raising price will decrease total revenue, since the percentage decrease in quantity demanded outweighs the price increase; lowering price would instead increase total revenue, since the large percentage gain in quantity sold more than compensates for the lower price per unit. A manager can apply the total revenue test directly without even calculating a precise elasticity number, simply by testing a small price change and observing whether total revenue rises or falls. For example, a seller of a product with few close substitutes, like a life-saving medication with inelastic demand, could profitably raise price, while a seller of a product like a specific soda brand, which faces elastic demand because buyers can easily switch brands, would generally see revenue fall if it raised price unilaterally. Pricing decisions should always also weigh costs, competitor reactions, and customer goodwill, not elasticity alone. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 3: Elasticity](chapters/03-elasticity/index.md)

### How would you use the MR = MC rule to decide whether a firm should expand or shut down production?

To decide whether to expand, compare the marginal revenue and marginal cost of the next unit the firm is considering producing: if marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost, expanding output by that unit adds to profit, and the firm should produce it; the firm should keep expanding output unit by unit until marginal revenue equals marginal cost, which is its profit-maximizing output level, and stop there, since producing further units beyond that point would cost more than they bring in. To decide whether to shut down entirely in the short run, the relevant comparison shifts to price versus average variable cost at that profit-maximizing output: if price exceeds average variable cost, even if it is below average total cost, the firm should keep operating, since it is covering its variable costs and recovering part of its fixed costs; only if price falls below average variable cost should the firm shut down temporarily, since continuing to operate would mean losing money on every unit beyond its unavoidable fixed costs. In the long run, the comparison instead should be price versus average total cost, since every cost becomes avoidable by exiting the industry if the firm cannot break even over the long haul. Working through these two separate comparisons, output level first, then operate-or-shutdown second, keeps the decision from becoming an all-or-nothing judgment call made on gut instinct. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 6: Perfect Competition](chapters/06-perfect-competition/index.md)

### How can game theory help you decide whether to trust a competitor's pricing signal?

Game theory encourages you to ask not whether a competitor's announced price or threat sounds credible, but whether it would actually be in that competitor's own rational self-interest to follow through on it once the moment to act actually arrives, which is exactly the logic behind identifying a non-credible threat. Model the situation as a sequential game: lay out your competitor's possible responses to your own pricing decision, and use backward induction to solve for what they would truly find optimal at that later stage, given the payoffs they would actually receive, rather than simply taking their stated intention at face value. For example, an incumbent firm might publicly threaten to start a costly price war against any new entrant, but if backward induction reveals that the price war would actually hurt the incumbent more than simply tolerating modest new competition, that threat is non-credible, and a rational entrant should discount it and enter anyway. Repeated interactions change this calculation: in a repeated game, a firm's credibility can be rebuilt over many rounds through consistent strategies like tit-for-tat, where sticking to a stated pricing policy round after round makes future threats and promises considerably more believable than in a single one-shot interaction. The practical takeaway is to always verify a rival's signal against their own incentives, not their words alone. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 10: Game Theory](chapters/10-game-theory/index.md)

### How can a nudge be used to encourage a specific behavior without banning any option?

A nudge is a feature of how choices are presented, such as a default option, designed to steer people's decisions in a particular direction without restricting any of the options actually available to them; the policy philosophy behind using nudges deliberately is called libertarian paternalism, favoring choices that are better for people while fully preserving their freedom to choose differently. To design an effective nudge, identify the behavior you want to encourage, then change the default or the presentation of choices so that the desired behavior requires no extra effort, while still leaving every original option fully available to anyone who actively wants it. For example, automatically enrolling new employees in a retirement savings plan, while still letting them opt out at any time, is a nudge: it leverages the common tendency toward inertia (and present bias toward current spending over future saving) to boost retirement saving rates substantially, without legally requiring anyone to participate. Similarly, placing healthier food options at eye level in a cafeteria, while still offering less healthy options elsewhere on the menu, nudges people toward better choices without removing the less healthy options entirely. The key design principle distinguishing a nudge from a mandate or ban is that the person retains complete freedom to opt out or choose otherwise; only the default and the ease of each path have changed. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 15: Behavioral Economics](chapters/15-behavioral-economics/index.md)

### How do you analyze whether a market is better served by more competition or by allowing economies of scale to consolidate it?

The analysis hinges on comparing the shape of the industry's long-run average cost curve to the size of the market. If a single firm's long-run average cost keeps falling over the entire relevant range of market demand, the industry exhibits natural monopoly conditions, meaning a single large firm can genuinely supply the market more cheaply per unit than several smaller competing firms could; in this case, forcing competition by breaking the firm into many smaller producers would raise average costs for everyone, even though it might appear to increase competition. A single electric grid serving a city is often cited as a natural monopoly for exactly this reason, since building duplicate sets of power lines would be far more costly than one network. If, instead, the minimum efficient scale is small relative to total market demand, meaning many firms can each individually reach their lowest average cost while the market still supports numerous competitors, then promoting competition through antitrust enforcement is more likely to benefit consumers, since competitive pressure will tend to push price down toward marginal cost without sacrificing cost efficiency. The practical move is therefore to estimate where minimum efficient scale falls relative to market size, since that ratio, not an ideological preference for or against competition, should drive the policy recommendation. [Chapter 7: Monopoly](chapters/07-monopoly/index.md)

### How should you weigh the trade-offs between a tariff and free trade when evaluating a trade policy?

A tariff, a tax imposed on imported goods, raises their price to domestic consumers, protects import-competing domestic producers and the workers they employ, and raises government revenue, but it also creates a tariff welfare loss, a reduction in the country's total surplus caused by distorting trade away from the efficient quantity of imports, beyond whatever revenue the tariff raises. Free trade maximizes a country's total gains from specializing according to comparative advantage, since it lets consumers buy at the lowest world price and lets domestic producers specialize in whatever they can produce most efficiently relative to other countries, but it also exposes certain groups, particularly workers in import-competing industries, to real and sometimes painful adjustment costs, a phenomenon the course calls trade winners and losers. Weighing this trade-off well requires being explicit about both efficiency and equity criteria rather than only one: on pure efficiency grounds, free trade nearly always generates larger total gains than a tariff, but a policymaker also has to weigh whether the losses borne by specific groups, such as displaced factory workers, are severe enough and concentrated enough to justify either temporary protection (as in the infant industry argument) or direct compensation through programs like trade adjustment assistance, rather than a blanket tariff that sacrifices overall efficiency to protect a narrower group. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 16: International Trade](chapters/16-international-trade/index.md)

### How do you analyze whether a given market failure is better addressed by a Pigouvian tax, a subsidy, or a quantity-based tool like cap-and-trade?

Start by identifying which direction the externality runs: a negative externality, where the market overproduces relative to the efficient quantity, calls for a Pigouvian tax, set equal to the marginal external cost, to discourage excess production; a positive externality, where the market underproduces, calls for a Pigouvian subsidy, set equal to the marginal external benefit, to encourage more of the activity. Once you know the direction, the choice between a price-based tool (a Pigouvian tax) and a quantity-based tool (cap-and-trade) typically comes down to which one you can set more confidently and how much uncertainty exists about costs. A Pigouvian tax is easier to use when you are confident about the per-unit external cost but uncertain about how costly it will be for different polluters to reduce their emissions, since a fixed tax lets each firm with a different marginal abatement cost choose its own response. Cap-and-trade is easier to use when you want certainty over the total quantity of pollution allowed, since the cap directly fixes the aggregate outcome, while still letting the market, through trading permits, find the cheapest way to distribute the required reductions among firms with different abatement costs. In practice, this means you should ask whether price certainty or quantity certainty matters more for the specific externality at hand before choosing between the two tools. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 13: Externalities and Public Goods](chapters/13-externalities-and-public-goods/index.md)

### How do you evaluate whether a monopoly's higher price is justified by its lower costs, as in the case of a natural monopoly?

Evaluating this requires comparing two separate effects that pull in opposite directions: the deadweight loss created by the monopoly restricting output and pricing above marginal cost, versus the genuine cost savings created by economies of scale that make a single large producer cheaper per unit than multiple smaller competing producers would be. For a natural monopoly, where long-run average cost keeps falling over the entire relevant range of market demand, forcing the market open to several competing firms would mean each firm operates at a smaller, less efficient scale, raising the average cost of production for everyone, even though competition would otherwise push price closer to marginal cost. The standard policy evaluation weighs this trade-off using regulation rather than simply accepting the unregulated monopoly outcome or forcing in competition: marginal-cost pricing regulation sets price equal to marginal cost to fully eliminate the deadweight loss, achieving allocative efficiency, but it can cause the firm financial losses if marginal cost lies below average total cost at that quantity, often requiring a government subsidy; average-cost pricing regulation instead sets price equal to average total cost, letting the firm break even without a subsidy, though output remains below the fully efficient level. Choosing between these regulatory approaches, rather than between monopoly and forced competition, is usually the more defensible evaluation for a genuine natural monopoly. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 7: Monopoly](chapters/07-monopoly/index.md)

### How would you evaluate the welfare effects of different types of price discrimination from a consumer's perspective?

Consumers are generally worse off under first-degree price discrimination, since a seller charging each buyer their own maximum willingness to pay captures the entirety of consumer surplus as profit, leaving buyers with none of the gains from trade they would keep in a single-price market, even though total output, and therefore total surplus, may actually be higher than under simple monopoly pricing because the seller no longer restricts output to protect a single high price. Second-degree price discrimination tends to have mixed effects: it can benefit high-usage or budget-conscious consumers who take advantage of bulk discounts or lower-cost product versions, while buyers who do not fit neatly into the offered menu may pay more than they would under a single uniform price. Third-degree price discrimination, charging different prices to identifiable groups, can genuinely benefit the group that receives the lower price, such as students paying less for movie tickets, while making the group charged the higher price worse off than under a single uniform price; whether total consumer welfare rises or falls overall depends on the specific elasticities of the different groups and the respective sizes of the group discounts versus group surcharges. A full evaluation should therefore distinguish which specific group of consumers is being asked about, rather than treating "consumers" as a single, uniformly affected group. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 7: Monopoly](chapters/07-monopoly/index.md)

### How would you design a policy proposal that addresses a specific negative externality using the tools from this course?

A strong policy proposal starts by clearly identifying the externality: who is the third party bearing the uncompensated cost, what is the activity generating it, and roughly how large is the marginal external cost per unit of that activity. Next, estimate the socially efficient quantity of the activity, the level where social marginal cost, the firm's private marginal cost plus the marginal external cost, equals social marginal benefit, and compare it to the current, inefficient market quantity to size the externality deadweight loss being created. From there, choose a policy tool and justify it explicitly: a Pigouvian tax set equal to the marginal external cost if you want price certainty and are comfortable letting the market determine the resulting quantity; a cap-and-trade system if you want quantity certainty and are willing to let the market determine the resulting price through permit trading; or, if transaction costs between the parties are low and property rights can be clearly assigned, consider whether a Coase theorem solution, letting the affected parties negotiate directly, might resolve the externality without government intervention at all. Finally, address likely objections using efficiency and equity criteria together: explain who bears the cost of your proposed policy, whether it might risk government failure (such as a weak tax being captured or watered down by lobbying), and why your chosen tool outperforms the alternatives for this specific externality. ## Advanced Topics

### How would you apply game theory concepts to analyze pricing behavior in an oligopoly?

Begin by identifying which specific oligopoly model best fits the competitive variable firms actually choose: if firms compete primarily by choosing output quantities, the Cournot model applies, where each firm picks a quantity based on a best-response function that accounts for how much its rival is expected to produce, and the resulting Cournot-Nash equilibrium typically yields a price between the competitive and monopoly levels. If firms compete primarily by choosing prices for an identical product, the Bertrand model applies instead, and strikingly, competitive price-cutting between even just two firms can drive price all the way down to marginal cost, the same outcome as perfect competition, a result known as the Bertrand paradox. If firms interact repeatedly over time rather than just once, you should also model the pricing game as a repeated game, since strategies like tit-for-tat can sustain tacit collusion, keeping prices above the competitive level for extended periods, something a single one-shot game cannot achieve. Finally, if firms believe rivals will match price cuts but not price increases, the kinked demand curve model helps explain price stability (firms are reluctant to change price in either direction) even when costs shift modestly, which is a useful complement to the Cournot and Bertrand frameworks for explaining real-world oligopoly pricing patterns observed across industries like airlines or wireless carriers. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 9: Oligopoly](chapters/09-oligopoly/index.md)

### How does information economics help explain why insurance markets and used-car markets suffer from similar problems despite being completely different industries?

Both markets share the same underlying structural problem, information asymmetry, even though the products being traded could not be more different. In the market for lemons, the classic used-car model, sellers of used cars know the true quality of their own car far better than buyers do, so buyers, unable to tell good cars from bad ones, offer only an average price, which discourages owners of genuinely good used cars from selling at all, leaving mostly low-quality "lemons" in the market. In health or auto insurance, buyers know their own risk level far better than the insurer does, so an insurer offering a single average premium attracts disproportionately higher-risk applicants, the same adverse selection dynamic, potentially spiraling toward an adverse selection death spiral as healthier participants exit and prices keep climbing. The common thread across both markets is that the less-informed party cannot distinguish quality (or risk) and therefore cannot price it correctly, which drives good-quality participants out of the market in both cases. Both markets also share remedies drawn from the same toolkit: signaling, where the informed party takes a costly action to credibly prove their quality (a seller providing a vehicle history report, or a healthy applicant submitting to a medical exam), and screening, where the less-informed party designs a menu of options that different types self-select into, revealing their private information indirectly. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 14: Information Economics](chapters/14-information-economics/index.md)

### How do the gains from international trade based on comparative advantage connect to the way factor markets determine wages domestically?

International trade theory, especially the Heckscher-Ohlin model, predicts that countries export goods relying heavily on the production factors they have in relative abundance and import goods relying on factors they have relatively less of, which directly affects domestic factor markets because trade changes the derived demand for labor and capital in each industry. When a country opens to trade and specializes according to comparative advantage, demand for labor rises in its expanding, comparative-advantage industries and falls in its contracting, import-competing industries, shifting each industry's labor demand curve and therefore the wage and employment level determined in that specific labor market, exactly the same marginal-revenue-product logic from the factor markets chapter, just driven by a change originating in trade policy rather than domestic technology. This is also the microeconomic root of trade winners and losers: workers whose skills are specific to an expanding export industry see rising labor demand and wages, while workers in a contracting import-competing industry see falling labor demand and wages, even though the country's total gains from trade are positive overall. Factor-price equalization theory takes this connection even further, predicting that as countries trade more freely, wages for similarly skilled workers across trading countries should tend to converge over time, since trade in goods functions as an indirect substitute for the cross-border movement of the underlying factors themselves. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 16: International Trade](chapters/16-international-trade/index.md)

### How would you evaluate whether behavioral economics findings like loss aversion and present bias justify "nudge" policies over traditional taxes and subsidies?

Evaluating this requires weighing what each policy tool assumes about human decision-making and comparing it to actual evidence about where people systematically deviate from the idealized, perfectly rational consumer assumed in classical models like consumer theory in Chapter 4. Traditional taxes and subsidies, such as a Pigouvian tax, work by changing the relative price of an option, which classical theory assumes is sufficient because rational consumers always respond correctly to price signals; but behavioral economics findings such as loss aversion, present bias, and bounded rationality show that people often respond inconsistently to logically equivalent price changes depending on how those changes are framed, and that people frequently act against their own long-run interests due to time-inconsistent preferences, like repeatedly postponing retirement saving despite planning not to. A nudge, by contrast, works directly on the cognitive bias or default behavior causing the mismatch, for instance addressing procrastination directly through automatic enrollment, rather than only indirectly through price. The honest evaluation is that nudges and price-based tools are not mutually exclusive but address different failure modes: nudges are particularly well suited to problems rooted in defaults, framing, and self-control, where changing a price might not even register strongly in someone's decision, while taxes and subsidies remain more directly suited to externality problems where the core issue is a missing or mispriced cost, not a cognitive bias. The strongest policies often combine both. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 15: Behavioral Economics](chapters/15-behavioral-economics/index.md)

### How would you evaluate the claim that free trade always makes a country better off, given that it can still create domestic winners and losers?

The claim is true in an important, specific sense and overstated in another: free trade reliably increases a country's total gains from trade, the aggregate size of the economic pie, because specializing according to comparative advantage lets the country, and its trading partners, collectively consume more than if each tried to produce everything domestically, a result the Ricardian trade model demonstrates rigorously. But "the country" is not a single decision-maker; it is made up of distinct groups, some of whom work in expanding export industries and clearly gain, and others who work in contracting import-competing industries and can be made genuinely worse off, even as the economy as a whole gains, exactly the trade winners and losers pattern the course highlights. Evaluating the claim fairly therefore requires separating the efficiency question (does total surplus rise? yes, in standard trade models) from the distributional question (does every individual gain? no, not without some further redistribution). This is why serious evaluations of trade policy pair free trade with explicit attention to the losers, for example through trade adjustment assistance programs that use part of the aggregate gains to compensate or retrain displaced workers, rather than assuming the aggregate gain alone settles the political or ethical question of whether a specific trade agreement is good policy for everyone affected by it. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 16: International Trade](chapters/16-international-trade/index.md)

### How would you design a complete policy response to climate change that draws on both externality theory and behavioral economics?

A complete design starts with the externality framework: carbon emissions impose a marginal external cost on the rest of society in the form of climate damage, so the core policy tool should be either a Pigouvian tax, a tax on each ton of carbon emitted set equal to the external cost, or a cap-and-trade system, which fixes the total quantity of allowed emissions and lets a market in tradable permits find the cheapest way to achieve that reduction across firms with different marginal abatement costs. On top of this efficiency-based foundation, a well-designed policy should also incorporate behavioral economics insights, since individual decisions about energy use, home insulation, or transportation choices are often driven by present bias (discounting future energy savings too heavily relative to today's upfront cost) and defaults (sticking with whatever energy plan or appliance setting came pre-installed) rather than a careful marginal-benefit-versus-marginal-cost calculation alone. Pairing the Pigouvian tax or cap-and-trade system with well-designed nudges, such as making energy-efficient options the default choice when purchasing appliances or enrolling in utility programs, while preserving consumer choice under libertarian paternalism, can push behavior further toward the efficient outcome than a price signal alone, especially for decisions where present bias causes consumers to systematically undervalue future costs and benefits relative to their true present value. Such a policy should also include transitional support, akin to trade adjustment assistance, for workers and regions economically dependent on high-carbon industries. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 13: Externalities and Public Goods](chapters/13-externalities-and-public-goods/index.md)

### How would you build an argument connecting market structure, game theory, and antitrust policy to explain why some industries attract more government scrutiny than others?

The argument starts with market structure: industries with few large firms, oligopolies, create strategic interdependence, meaning each firm's pricing and output decisions depend directly on its rivals' decisions, unlike perfectly competitive or even monopolistically competitive industries made up of many small, independent decision-makers. Game theory then explains why this structural feature specifically worries antitrust regulators: a small number of interacting firms can potentially reach a Nash equilibrium involving tacit or explicit collusion, especially in a repeated game where strategies like tit-for-tat allow firms to sustain cooperative, above-competitive pricing over many rounds without ever needing a formal, provably illegal agreement. Measures like the concentration ratio and the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index give regulators quantifiable tools to flag exactly which industries have concentrated enough market structure for this strategic dynamic to be a realistic concern, which is why antitrust policy, including oligopoly-specific antitrust policy and historical tools like the Sherman Antitrust Act, focuses disproportionate scrutiny on concentrated industries and proposed mergers that would increase concentration further, while leaving fragmented, monopolistically competitive industries like restaurants or hair salons largely unscrutinized. The connecting thread across all three layers is that structure (few firms) enables a strategic dynamic (interdependence, modeled by game theory) that threatens the policy goal (competitive pricing), which is exactly why antitrust enforcement targets market structure and firm conduct together rather than either one alone. [Glossary](glossary.md). [Chapter 9: Oligopoly](chapters/09-oligopoly/index.md)

### How would you construct an original numerical example showing how asymmetric information could cause a monopsony-like labor market to also suffer from adverse selection?

Consider a single rural hospital that is the only major employer of nurses in its region, making it a monopsony in the local labor market: suppose the competitive wage for nurses with observable average skill would be $35 per hour, but because the hospital is the sole buyer, it follows the monopsony hiring rule, hiring only up to the point where the marginal revenue product of the next nurse equals the marginal cost of labor, resulting in a lower posted wage of, say, $28 per hour and fewer nurses hired than a competitive market would support. Now add an information problem: suppose nursing skill varies, and the hospital cannot fully observe each individual applicant's true skill level before hiring, only applicants' own private information about themselves. At a wage of $28, the most skilled nurses, who could earn more in a competitive market elsewhere or in a different profession entirely, are the ones most likely to decline the position or leave quickly, while lower-skilled nurses, with fewer outside options, are more likely to accept and stay, a selection pattern directly analogous to the market for lemons. The hospital's monopsony wage-setting, meant simply to capture monopsony profit through a lower wage, inadvertently also degrades the average quality of its workforce over time, layering an adverse selection cost on top of the standard monopsony inefficiency of hiring fewer workers than the efficient quantity.
