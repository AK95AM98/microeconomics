# Quiz: Supply and Demand

Test your understanding of the laws of demand and supply, market equilibrium, and consumer and producer surplus with these review questions.

---

#### 1. According to the law of demand, holding all else constant (ceteris paribus), what happens to quantity demanded when the price of a good rises?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Quantity demanded also rises, because a higher price signals higher quality
2. Quantity demanded falls, because the good becomes relatively more expensive and buyers' purchasing power declines
3. Quantity demanded stays the same, since demand depends only on income
4. The entire demand curve shifts to the left
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The law of demand states that, ceteris paribus, quantity demanded and price move in opposite directions, driven by both the substitution effect (the good becomes relatively more expensive) and the income effect (real purchasing power falls). Option A reverses the relationship, option C ignores the role of price entirely, and option D confuses a price-driven movement along the curve with a shift of the entire curve.

    **Concept Tested:** Law of Demand

---

#### 2. According to the law of supply, what is the relationship between a good's price and the quantity supplied, ceteris paribus?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. As price rises, producers are willing to supply less, since higher prices reduce profit margins
2. As price rises, quantity supplied is unaffected, since production decisions depend only on input costs
3. As price rises, quantity supplied rises, because higher prices make production more profitable
4. As price falls, quantity supplied rises, inducing new firms to enter the market
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The law of supply holds that price and quantity supplied move in the same direction: higher prices make production more profitable and induce firms to incur higher marginal costs to supply more. Option A reverses the true relationship, option B wrongly claims price has no effect, and option D reverses the direction of the price change described.

    **Concept Tested:** Law of Supply

---

#### 3. What defines market equilibrium?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The price and quantity at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears
2. The price at which producer surplus is maximized regardless of consumer surplus
3. The lowest price at which any seller in the market is willing to sell
4. A price ceiling set by government regulation to protect consumers
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Market equilibrium occurs where the demand and supply curves intersect — quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, leaving no unsatisfied demand and no unsold surplus. Option B incorrectly focuses on only one side of the market, option C describes a single seller's minimum acceptable price rather than a market-clearing outcome, and option D describes a government policy, not a market outcome.

    **Concept Tested:** Market Equilibrium

---

#### 4. How is consumer surplus best defined?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The minimum price a seller would be willing to accept for a good
2. The total revenue a firm earns from selling a good
3. The combined surplus received by both buyers and sellers in a market
4. The difference between the maximum price a buyer is willing to pay and the price the buyer actually pays
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Consumer surplus measures the gain buyers receive from paying less than their maximum willingness to pay, summed across all buyers; graphically it is the area above the price and below the demand curve. Option A describes a seller's minimum acceptable price (related to producer surplus), option B describes total revenue, and option C describes total surplus, which combines both consumer and producer surplus.

    **Concept Tested:** Consumer Surplus

---

#### 5. A rise in the price of coffee itself causes consumers to buy less coffee. How should this change be correctly described?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A decrease in demand, since the entire demand curve shifts left
2. A movement along the existing demand curve, representing a change in quantity demanded rather than a shift in demand
3. An increase in demand, since higher prices typically raise seller revenue
4. A shift in the supply curve, since a price change affects sellers too
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Because the change is caused by the price of the good itself, this is a movement along a fixed demand curve — a change in quantity demanded — not a shift of the curve, which only occurs when a non-price determinant of demand changes. Option A and C incorrectly describe this as a shift, and option D misattributes a demand-side price response to the supply curve.

    **Concept Tested:** Quantity Demanded

---

#### 6. Suppose consumer income rises, and as a result people buy fewer frozen dinners because they can now afford fresher, higher-quality meals. Frozen dinners in this example are best classified as:

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A substitute good
2. A complement good
3. An inferior good, since demand falls as income rises
4. A normal good, since demand responds to changes in income
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. An inferior good is one for which quantity demanded falls as income rises, because consumers switch to preferred alternatives once they can afford them — exactly the pattern described for frozen dinners. Options A and B describe relationships between two different goods' prices, not income effects, and option D is too vague since normal goods move in the *same* direction as income, the opposite of what is described.

    **Concept Tested:** Inferior Good

#### 7. Butter and margarine are substitute goods. If the price of butter rises, what happens in the market for margarine?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Demand for margarine increases, shifting its demand curve to the right
2. Demand for margarine decreases, shifting its demand curve to the left
3. Quantity demanded of margarine falls, a movement along its own demand curve
4. Demand for margarine is unaffected, since the two goods are sold in separate markets
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Because butter and margarine are substitutes, a higher price for butter makes margarine relatively more attractive, increasing demand for margarine at every price and shifting its demand curve rightward. Option B describes the opposite direction, option C mistakenly treats this as a movement along margarine's own curve rather than a shift caused by another good's price, and option D ignores the substitute relationship entirely.

    **Concept Tested:** Substitute Goods

---

#### 8. When the market price of a good is below its equilibrium price, what tends to happen?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A surplus develops as sellers accumulate unsold inventory
2. Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at that price
3. The market has already reached long-run equilibrium
4. A shortage develops, and competition among buyers bids the price upward toward equilibrium
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. A price below equilibrium creates excess demand — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied — so buyers compete for the limited quantity available, bidding the price up until it returns to equilibrium. Options A and B both describe a surplus, which instead occurs when price is *above* equilibrium, and option C is incorrect because a price below equilibrium is, by definition, not yet at the market-clearing point.

    **Concept Tested:** Market Shortage

---

#### 9. Given the demand and supply equations $Q_d = 90 - 3P$ and $Q_s = 10 + 2P$ for a good, what is the equilibrium price?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $14
2. $16
3. $18
4. $20
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Setting $Q_d = Q_s$ gives $90 - 3P = 10 + 2P$, so $80 = 5P$ and $P^* = 16$. The other options result from common algebra slips, such as mis-combining the coefficients or constants, and do not satisfy the equilibrium condition when substituted back into both equations.

    **Concept Tested:** Equilibrium Price

---

#### 10. Suppose both demand and supply for a good increase simultaneously (the demand curve shifts right and the supply curve shifts right). Which outcome is certain?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Equilibrium price definitely rises
2. Equilibrium price definitely falls
3. Equilibrium quantity definitely rises, while the effect on price is ambiguous
4. Equilibrium quantity definitely falls, while price definitely rises
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. When both curves shift right, both effects push equilibrium quantity up, so the increase in quantity is certain; however, the demand increase pushes price up while the supply increase pushes price down, so the net effect on price depends on which shift is larger and cannot be determined without more information. Options A and B wrongly claim certainty about price, and option D incorrectly predicts quantity would fall.

    **Concept Tested:** Market Equilibrium
