## 2.1 Markets

A **market** is any arrangement that facilitates the exchange of a good or service between buyers and sellers. Markets can be physical (a farmers' market), virtual (Amazon), or decentralized (the labor market). The **price** that emerges in a market coordinates the decisions of buyers and sellers without any central authority directing them.

!!! example "Markets everywhere"
    - The market for Minneapolis apartment rentals
    - The market for computer science graduates
    - The New York Stock Exchange
    - The global market for crude oil

---

## 2.2 Demand

### The Law of Demand

**Demand** refers to the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that consumers are *willing and able* to buy, holding all else equal.

**The Law of Demand:** When the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls (and vice versa), *ceteris paribus*.

$$Q_d = f(P, \overline{I}, \overline{P_r}, \overline{\text{Tastes}}, \overline{E}, \overline{N})$$

where $P$ is price (variable) and the bars denote factors held constant.

The **demand curve** is a downward-sloping line (or curve) showing the quantity demanded at each price.

### Why Does Demand Slope Downward?

Two effects explain the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded:

1. **Substitution effect:** When the price of coffee rises, consumers substitute toward tea. The good becomes relatively more expensive.
2. **Income effect:** When the price of coffee rises, consumers' purchasing power falls — they can afford less of everything, including coffee.

### The Demand Schedule and Curve

Consider the market for gasoline in Minneapolis:

| Price ($/gallon) | Quantity Demanded (million gallons/month) |
|---|---|
| \$2.00 | 60 |
| \$3.00 | 50 |
| \$4.00 | 40 |
| \$5.00 | 30 |
| \$6.00 | 20 |

Plotting price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis gives a downward-sloping demand curve.

### Movements Along vs. Shifts of the Demand Curve

**Movement along the demand curve:** Caused only by a change in the **price of the good itself**. This is a change in *quantity demanded*.

**Shift of the demand curve:** Caused by a change in any *other* factor that affects demand. The entire curve moves left (decrease) or right (increase).

### Determinants of Demand (Demand Shifters)

| Determinant | Increase in Demand | Decrease in Demand |
|---|---|---|
| **Income** (normal good) | Income rises | Income falls |
| **Income** (inferior good) | Income falls | Income rises |
| **Price of a substitute** | Substitute price rises | Substitute price falls |
| **Price of a complement** | Complement price falls | Complement price rises |
| **Consumer tastes** | Good becomes more popular | Good becomes less popular |
| **Expectations** (future price ↑) | Buy more now | Buy less now |
| **Number of buyers** | More buyers | Fewer buyers |

!!! example "Substitutes and complements"
    - **Substitutes:** Butter and margarine. If the price of butter rises, demand for margarine increases (the demand curve for margarine shifts right).
    - **Complements:** Cars and gasoline. If the price of gasoline rises, demand for large cars decreases (the demand curve for large cars shifts left).

!!! example "Normal vs. inferior goods"
    - **Normal goods:** As income rises, demand rises. Example: restaurant meals, new cars.
    - **Inferior goods:** As income rises, demand falls. Example: instant noodles, used cars. (People switch to better substitutes when they can afford it.)

## 2.3 Supply

### The Law of Supply

**Supply** refers to the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity that producers are *willing and able* to sell, holding all else equal.

**The Law of Supply:** When the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises (and vice versa), *ceteris paribus*.

The **supply curve** is an upward-sloping line (or curve) showing the quantity supplied at each price.

### Why Does Supply Slope Upward?

- Higher prices make production more profitable, inducing existing firms to expand output and attracting new firms into the market.
- At higher prices, firms are willing to incur higher marginal costs to supply more units.

### The Supply Schedule and Curve

| Price ($/gallon) | Quantity Supplied (million gallons/month) |
|---|---|
| \$2.00 | 20 |
| \$3.00 | 30 |
| \$4.00 | 40 |
| \$5.00 | 50 |
| \$6.00 | 60 |

### Determinants of Supply (Supply Shifters)

| Determinant | Increase in Supply | Decrease in Supply |
|---|---|---|
| **Input prices** (e.g., wages, materials) | Input costs fall | Input costs rise |
| **Technology** | Productivity improves | Productivity falls |
| **Number of sellers** | More sellers enter | Sellers exit |
| **Government policy** | Subsidy given | Tax imposed |
| **Expectations** (future price ↑) | Withhold supply now | — |
| **Prices of related goods in production** | Alternative good price falls | Alternative good price rises |

!!! example "Technology and supply"
    The development of hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") dramatically lowered the cost of extracting natural gas. This shifted the supply curve for natural gas rightward — producers were willing to supply more at every price, driving down the market price.

## 2.4 Market Equilibrium

### Finding Equilibrium

**Market equilibrium** occurs where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. At the **equilibrium price** ($P^*$), the market **clears** — there is no unsatisfied demand and no unsold surplus.

$$Q_d(P^*) = Q_s(P^*) \implies \text{market equilibrium}$$

Using our gasoline example:

| Price | $Q_d$ | $Q_s$ | Surplus / Shortage |
|---|---|---|---|
| \$2.00 | 60 | 20 | Shortage of 40 |
| \$3.00 | 50 | 30 | Shortage of 20 |
| **\$4.00** | **40** | **40** | **Equilibrium** |
| \$5.00 | 30 | 50 | Surplus of 20 |
| \$6.00 | 20 | 60 | Surplus of 40 |

The equilibrium is $P^* = \$4.00$, $Q^* = 40$ million gallons.

### Surplus and Shortage

**Surplus (excess supply):** When price is *above* equilibrium, $Q_s > Q_d$. Unsold goods accumulate; sellers lower prices. The market self-corrects downward toward $P^*$.

**Shortage (excess demand):** When price is *below* equilibrium, $Q_d > Q_s$. Buyers compete for scarce goods; prices are bid up. The market self-corrects upward toward $P^*$.

!!! tip "The market as a coordinating mechanism"
    Prices serve as signals. A price above equilibrium signals "too much supply — cut back." A price below equilibrium signals "too much demand — produce more." No central planner is needed.

### Mathematical Example

Suppose the demand and supply equations for a good are:

$$Q_d = 100 - 2P$$
$$Q_s = 10 + 3P$$

**Finding equilibrium:**

$$Q_d = Q_s$$
$$100 - 2P = 10 + 3P$$
$$90 = 5P$$
$$P^* = 18$$
$$Q^* = 100 - 2(18) = 64$$

The equilibrium price is **\$18** and equilibrium quantity is **64 units**.

## 2.5 Changes in Equilibrium

When supply or demand shifts, the equilibrium changes. Use the following three-step method:

**Step 1:** Draw the initial supply and demand curves and mark the initial equilibrium.  
**Step 2:** Determine which curve shifts, and in which direction.  
**Step 3:** Find the new equilibrium and describe what happened to $P$ and $Q$.

### Case 1: Demand Increases

*Example: A hot summer increases demand for ice cream.*

- Demand curve shifts **right**.
- At the old price, there is a shortage.
- Price is bid up → equilibrium price **rises**.
- Higher price induces more production → equilibrium quantity **rises**.

**Result: Both P and Q rise.**

### Case 2: Demand Decreases

*Example: A health study links red meat to cancer, reducing beef demand.*

- Demand curve shifts **left**.
- At the old price, there is a surplus.
- Price falls → equilibrium price **falls**.
- Lower price reduces production → equilibrium quantity **falls**.

**Result: Both P and Q fall.**

### Case 3: Supply Increases

*Example: Better corn seed technology increases corn supply.*

- Supply curve shifts **right**.
- At the old price, there is a surplus.
- Price falls → equilibrium price **falls**.
- Lower price increases quantity demanded → equilibrium quantity **rises**.

**Result: P falls, Q rises.**

### Case 4: Supply Decreases

*Example: A drought destroys part of the wheat crop.*

- Supply curve shifts **left**.
- At the old price, there is a shortage.
- Price rises → equilibrium price **rises**.
- Higher price reduces quantity demanded → equilibrium quantity **falls**.

**Result: P rises, Q falls.**

### Summary of Equilibrium Effects

| Event | Demand | Supply | $\Delta P^*$ | $\Delta Q^*$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand increases | Shifts right | Unchanged | ↑ | ↑ |
| Demand decreases | Shifts left | Unchanged | ↓ | ↓ |
| Supply increases | Unchanged | Shifts right | ↓ | ↑ |
| Supply decreases | Unchanged | Shifts left | ↑ | ↓ |
| Both increase | Shifts right | Shifts right | ? | ↑↑ |
| Demand ↑, Supply ↓ | Shifts right | Shifts left | ↑↑ | ? |

!!! warning "Simultaneous shifts"
    When both curves shift, the effect on price or quantity may be ambiguous without knowing the relative magnitudes. For example, if demand and supply both increase, quantity definitely rises — but price could rise, fall, or stay the same depending on which shift dominates.

## 2.6 Consumer and Producer Surplus

### Consumer Surplus

**Consumer surplus** is the difference between what consumers are *willing to pay* for a good and what they *actually pay*.

$$\text{Consumer Surplus} = \text{Willingness to Pay} - \text{Price Paid}$$

Graphically, consumer surplus is the area **above the price** and **below the demand curve**.

!!! example "Ticket example"
    Three fans value a Taylor Swift concert ticket as follows: Fan A: \$300, Fan B: \$200, Fan C: \$100. If tickets sell for \$150, only Fan A and Fan B buy. Their consumer surpluses are:
    - Fan A: \$300 − \$150 = \$150
    - Fan B: \$200 − \$150 = \$50
    - Total Consumer Surplus = \$200

### Producer Surplus

**Producer surplus** is the difference between the price producers receive and the minimum price they would have accepted.

$$\text{Producer Surplus} = \text{Price Received} - \text{Minimum Acceptable Price}$$

Graphically, producer surplus is the area **below the price** and **above the supply curve**.

### Total Surplus and Market Efficiency

**Total surplus = Consumer surplus + Producer surplus**

A competitive market maximizes total surplus — it is **allocatively efficient**. No reallocation could make someone better off without making someone else worse off (this is Pareto efficiency).

!!! note "The invisible hand"
    Adam Smith (1776) observed that individuals pursuing their own self-interest in competitive markets are led by an "invisible hand" to promote the public interest. The supply-and-demand model formalizes this insight: competitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus.

## Practice Problems

### Problem 1 — Shifts in Demand

For each event, state whether demand for the good in **bold** increases, decreases, or is unchanged. Also state whether this is a movement along the demand curve or a shift.

**a)** The price of **butter** rises. What happens to demand for **margarine** (a substitute)?  
**b)** Income rises. What happens to demand for **instant ramen noodles** (an inferior good)?  
**c)** The price of **coffee** rises. What happens to demand for **coffee** itself?  
**d)** A new study finds that **red wine** reduces heart disease risk.  
**e)** The price of **printers** falls. What happens to demand for **printer ink**?

??? success "Solution"
    **a)** Demand for margarine **increases** (shifts right) — butter is a substitute; its higher price makes margarine relatively attractive.

    **b)** Demand for instant ramen **decreases** (shifts left) — inferior good; higher income reduces demand.

    **c)** The quantity demanded of coffee **falls** (movement along the demand curve) — this is a price change of the good itself, not a shift.

    **d)** Demand for red wine **increases** (shifts right) — tastes change in favor of the good.

    **e)** Demand for printer ink **increases** (shifts right) — printers and ink are complements; cheaper printers mean more printers sold, so more ink needed.

### Problem 2 — Market Equilibrium

The market for apartments in a college town has:

$$Q_d = 1200 - 3P$$
$$Q_s = 300 + 2P$$

where $Q$ is the number of apartments and $P$ is monthly rent.

**a)** Find the equilibrium rent and quantity.  
**b)** At a rent of \$200/month, is there a surplus or shortage? By how much?  
**c)** At a rent of \$240/month, is there a surplus or shortage? By how much?

??? success "Solution"
    **a)** Set $Q_d = Q_s$:
    
    $$1200 - 3P = 300 + 2P$$
    $$900 = 5P$$
    $$P^* = \$180, \quad Q^* = 1200 - 3(180) = 660 \text{ apartments}$$

    **b)** At P = \$200:
    - $Q_d = 1200 - 3(200) = 600$
    - $Q_s = 300 + 2(200) = 700$
    - **Surplus of 100 apartments** (quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded)

    **c)** At P = \$240:
    - $Q_d = 1200 - 3(240) = 480$
    - $Q_s = 300 + 2(240) = 780$
    - **Surplus of 300 apartments**

### Problem 3 — Equilibrium Changes

In the market for gasoline, explain the effect on the equilibrium price and quantity of each event. State clearly which curve shifts, in which direction, and what happens to $P^*$ and $Q^*$.

**a)** The price of crude oil (a key input) rises sharply.  
**b)** Higher fuel efficiency standards cause consumers to drive less.  
**c)** A new refinery opens, and simultaneously, the price of electric vehicles falls (reducing demand for gasoline).

??? success "Solution"
    **a)** Supply decreases (shifts left) — higher input cost.
    - $P^*$ **rises**, $Q^*$ **falls**.

    **b)** Demand decreases (shifts left) — consumers want less gasoline at every price.
    - $P^*$ **falls**, $Q^*$ **falls**.

    **c)** Supply increases (new refinery → shifts right) AND Demand decreases (cheaper EVs reduce gas demand → shifts left).
    - Both shifts push $Q^*$ **down** (unambiguously).
    - Effect on $P^*$ is **ambiguous** — supply increase pushes price down, demand decrease also pushes price down, so $P^*$ **falls** (in this case both effects reinforce each other).

### Problem 4 — Consumer and Producer Surplus

The supply and demand for a good are:

$$Q_d = 80 - P$$
$$Q_s = 20 + P$$

**a)** Find equilibrium P* and Q*.  
**b)** Calculate consumer surplus at equilibrium.  
**c)** Calculate producer surplus at equilibrium.  
**d)** What is total surplus?

??? success "Solution"
    **a)** Set $Q_d = Q_s$: $80 - P = 20 + P \Rightarrow 60 = 2P \Rightarrow P^* = 30, \; Q^* = 50$

    **b)** Consumer surplus is the area of the triangle above the price and below the demand curve.
    
    The demand curve hits the vertical axis when $Q_d = 0$: $0 = 80 - P \Rightarrow P = 80$.
    
    $$\text{CS} = \frac{1}{2} \times (80 - 30) \times 50 = \frac{1}{2} \times 50 \times 50 = \$1{,}250$$

    **c)** Producer surplus is the area of the triangle below the price and above the supply curve.
    
    The supply curve hits the vertical axis when $Q_s = 0$: $0 = 20 + P \Rightarrow P = -20$. Wait — the supply curve starts where $Q_s = 0$, so: $20 + P = 0 \Rightarrow P = -20$. But negative prices don't make sense here. The minimum supply price (the y-intercept of supply when we solve for P) is: the supply curve starts supplying when price is such that $Q_s > 0$, i.e., when $P > -20$. At $P = 0$, $Q_s = 20$.
    
    More precisely: solving $Q_s = 20 + P$ for P: $P = Q_s - 20$. The supply curve (as $P$ as a function of $Q$) is $P = Q - 20$. It intercepts the horizontal axis (P=0) at Q=20, and the P-axis at P=-20. Since we can't have negative prices in this market context, the supply curve effectively starts at Q=20 when P=0.
    
    Producer surplus = area below P* = 30 and above the supply curve, from Q=0 to Q=50. Since the supply "curve" P = Q - 20 intersects P=0 at Q=20 and P=30 at Q=50:
    
    $$\text{PS} = \frac{1}{2} \times (30 - (-20)) \times 50 = \frac{1}{2} \times 50 \times 50 = \$1{,}250$$
    
    Alternatively, using the triangular area with base 50 (from Q=0 to Q=50) and height = 30 - (-20) = 50: PS = ½ × 50 × 50 = \$1,250.

    **d)** Total surplus = CS + PS = \$1,250 + \$1,250 = **\$2,500**.

### Problem 5 — Real World Application

In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic caused the demand for hand sanitizer to increase dramatically. At the same time, some producers switched from producing other goods to producing hand sanitizer (supply increased). 

**a)** What happened to the equilibrium quantity of hand sanitizer? Explain.  
**b)** Is the effect on equilibrium price certain or ambiguous? Explain.  
**c)** During the pandemic, many stores ran out of hand sanitizer despite producers ramping up. Is this consistent with the supply-and-demand model? What does it suggest about prices?

??? success "Solution"
    **a)** Equilibrium quantity **definitely increased** — both the demand increase and the supply increase push quantity upward. The two effects reinforce each other.

    **b)** The effect on price is **ambiguous**. The demand increase pushes price up; the supply increase pushes price down. The net effect depends on which shift was larger.

    **c)** Running out of stock is a **shortage** — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price. This is consistent with supply-and-demand theory when price is held **below** equilibrium. Many retailers chose not to raise prices (for ethical or reputational reasons), so prices did not rise to clear the market. This suggests that the equilibrium price for hand sanitizer rose substantially — but price controls (informal or formal) prevented prices from reaching equilibrium.
