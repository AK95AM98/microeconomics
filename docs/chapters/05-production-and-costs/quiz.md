# Quiz: Production and Costs

Test your understanding of production functions, marginal and average product, short-run and long-run costs, and economies of scale with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What does a production function describe?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The maximum quantity of output a firm can produce from a given combination of inputs, given current technology
2. The minimum cost required to produce a given quantity of output
3. The relationship between a firm's price and its quantity supplied
4. The rate at which a firm substitutes capital for labor while keeping output constant
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A production function, $Q = f(L,K)$, is a purely technological relationship describing the maximum output obtainable from given quantities of inputs; it says nothing yet about costs or prices. Option B describes a cost-minimization concept rather than the production function itself, option C describes a supply curve, and option D describes a different input-substitution concept not covered by the basic production function.

    **Concept Tested:** Production Function

---

#### 2. Which statement correctly distinguishes a fixed cost from a variable cost?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Fixed costs rise with output while variable costs do not
2. Fixed costs are always larger in dollar terms than variable costs
3. Fixed costs only exist in the long run, while variable costs only exist in the short run
4. Fixed costs do not change with the quantity of output produced, while variable costs do
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Fixed costs, like a truck lease, must be paid regardless of output level, while variable costs, like ingredients, rise directly with the quantity produced. Option A reverses the definitions, option B makes an unsupported claim about relative size that is not part of either definition, and option C incorrectly ties fixed and variable costs to different time horizons rather than to how they respond to output.

    **Concept Tested:** Fixed Cost

---

#### 3. Which best describes the "short run" in production theory?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A period in which all inputs, including plant size, can be adjusted
2. A period in which at least one input, typically capital or plant size, is fixed while other inputs can be adjusted
3. A calendar period lasting less than one year, by definition
4. A period in which a firm cannot adjust any of its inputs
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The short run is defined by at least one fixed input, usually plant size or capital, while the firm can still adjust variable inputs like labor; it is a planning horizon, not a fixed calendar length. Option A describes the long run, option C incorrectly imposes a specific time length (the chapter notes the short run can be a decade for a power plant or months for a food truck), and option D describes a firm with no flexibility at all, which is too extreme.

    **Concept Tested:** Short Run

---

#### 4. In the food-truck example, marginal product rises from worker 1 to worker 2, then falls afterward. What does this imply about average product between those points?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Average product is falling throughout, since marginal product eventually declines
2. Average product is unrelated to marginal product
3. Average product reaches zero whenever marginal product declines
4. Average product rises as long as marginal product exceeds average product, and falls once marginal product drops below it
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Marginal product "pulls" average product: when $MP_L > AP_L$, an above-average worker raises the average, so average product rises; once $MP_L < AP_L$, it falls. Option A incorrectly claims average product falls even while marginal product is still rising and above the average, option B denies a well-established mathematical relationship, and option C confuses a declining marginal product with one that has reached zero.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Product

---

#### 5. Why does average fixed cost (AFC) always fall continuously as output increases?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because a constant fixed-cost amount is divided over an increasing quantity of output, spreading the fixed cost more thinly
2. Because variable costs decline as output rises
3. Because fixed costs themselves decrease as more units are produced
4. Because marginal cost always lies below average fixed cost
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Since $AFC = FC/Q$ and the numerator (fixed cost) never changes while the denominator (output) grows, AFC must fall continuously as output rises — this is a purely arithmetic result. Option B confuses AFC with average variable cost, option C incorrectly claims fixed costs themselves shrink, and option D makes an unrelated and unsupported claim about marginal cost's relationship to AFC.

    **Concept Tested:** Average Fixed Cost

---

#### 6. What best explains "economies of scale" along the long-run average cost curve?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Average cost rises as output rises due to coordination and bureaucracy problems
2. Average cost stays constant across all output levels regardless of plant size
3. Average cost falls as output rises, due to factors like specialization, bulk discounts, and spreading fixed costs like R&D over more units
4. Average cost depends only on labor costs, not on the scale of production
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Economies of scale occur on the downward-sloping portion of the long-run average cost curve, where larger output allows specialization, bulk-purchasing discounts, and more efficient spreading of costs like R&D. Option A describes diseconomies of scale instead, option B describes constant returns to scale, and option D ignores the many non-labor factors, such as equipment efficiency and purchasing power, that drive economies of scale.

    **Concept Tested:** Economies of Scale

#### 7. A firm's variable cost schedule is: $Q=0$, $VC=\$0$; $Q=1$, $VC=\$180$; $Q=2$, $VC=\$330$; $Q=3$, $VC=\$450$; $Q=4$, $VC=\$600$. Fixed cost is $300. What is the marginal cost of the 3rd unit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $450
2. $750
3. $150
4. $120
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Marginal cost equals the change in total (or variable) cost from one more unit: $MC_3 = VC_3 - VC_2 = 450 - 330 = \$120$. Option A is the total variable cost at $Q=3$ rather than the marginal cost, option B incorrectly adds fixed cost into the marginal calculation, and option C is the marginal cost of the 4th unit, not the 3rd.

    **Concept Tested:** Short-Run Marginal Cost

---

#### 8. Using the same cost schedule (fixed cost $300), what is average total cost at $Q=4$?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $225
2. $150
3. $600
4. $900
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Total cost at $Q=4$ is $TC = FC + VC = 300 + 600 = \$900$, so $ATC = TC/Q = 900/4 = \$225$. Option B divides only the variable cost by output while ignoring fixed cost, and options C and D both report total cost figures rather than dividing by quantity to get a per-unit average.

    **Concept Tested:** Average Total Cost

---

#### 9. A firm's total cost function is $TC(Q) = 400 + 30Q + 3Q^2$. What is the marginal cost at $Q=5$?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $30
2. $90
3. $60
4. $150
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Differentiating total cost gives $MC(Q) = 30 + 6Q$; evaluated at $Q=5$, $MC = 30 + 6(5) = \$60$. Option A is just the constant marginal term without the variable component, option B results from an incorrect coefficient, and option D overstates the result by miscomputing the derivative.

    **Concept Tested:** Short-Run Marginal Cost

#### 10. An industry has a minimum efficient scale that is very large relative to total market demand — only one or two firms can produce at the lowest point of the long-run average cost curve. Based on this, what market structure is this industry most likely to tend toward?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Many small competing firms, since a large minimum efficient scale always implies perfect competition
2. A market with only a few large firms, or even a natural monopoly, since few firms can operate at efficient scale
3. A monopolistic competition structure with many differentiated firms
4. An oligopoly is impossible whenever minimum efficient scale is large relative to demand
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. When the minimum efficient scale is large relative to market demand, only a small number of firms can produce at the lowest point on the long-run average cost curve, pushing the industry toward a few large firms or a natural monopoly, as with electric utilities. Option A describes the opposite case (small MES relative to demand), option C describes an unrelated market structure typically associated with small MES and product differentiation, and option D is false since a large MES is actually a common cause of oligopoly, not a barrier to it.

    **Concept Tested:** Minimum Efficient Scale
