# Quiz: Elasticity

Test your understanding of price elasticity of demand and supply, the total revenue test, and income and cross-price elasticity with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What does the price elasticity of demand measure?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price
2. The percentage change in price divided by the percentage change in quantity demanded
3. The absolute change in quantity demanded caused by a one-dollar change in price
4. The ratio of total revenue to total cost at a given price
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Price elasticity of demand is defined as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, which produces a unit-free number that can be compared across very different goods. Option B inverts the ratio, option C describes a non-percentage, unit-dependent measure (closer to slope than elasticity), and option D describes an unrelated financial ratio.

    **Concept Tested:** Price Elasticity of Demand

---

#### 2. Which condition describes "perfectly inelastic demand"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Quantity demanded rises by a larger percentage than price falls
2. Any price increase above the current level drives quantity demanded to zero
3. Quantity demanded changes by exactly the same percentage as price
4. Quantity demanded does not change at all, regardless of the price charged
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Perfectly inelastic demand means $E_d = 0$: quantity demanded stays completely fixed no matter how price changes, as with a patient's life-saving medication dose. Option A describes elastic demand, option B describes perfectly elastic demand (the opposite case), and option C describes unit-elastic demand.

    **Concept Tested:** Perfectly Inelastic Demand

---

#### 3. According to the Total Revenue Test, if demand is elastic, a price increase causes:

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Total revenue to rise, since higher prices generate more income per unit sold
2. Total revenue to fall, since the percentage drop in quantity demanded outweighs the percentage rise in price
3. Total revenue to remain unchanged, since elastic demand exactly offsets any price change
4. Total revenue to rise only if supply is also elastic
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. When demand is elastic ($\lvert E_d\rvert > 1$), the percentage decrease in quantity demanded is larger than the percentage increase in price, so total revenue ($P \times Q$) falls. Option A describes the inelastic case, option C describes the unit-elastic case, and option D incorrectly brings in supply, which is irrelevant to this demand-side revenue relationship.

    **Concept Tested:** Total Revenue Test

---

#### 4. A household's restaurant spending rises by 16% when its income rises by only 8%. Based on income elasticity, restaurant meals for this household are best classified as:

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. An inferior good
2. A good with inelastic supply
3. A complement to income itself
4. A luxury good, since income elasticity exceeds one
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Income elasticity here is $16\%/8\% = 2.0$, which exceeds 1, classifying restaurant meals as a luxury (income-elastic) good whose demand grows faster than income. Option A would require a negative income elasticity, option B confuses income elasticity with supply elasticity, and option C is not a meaningful classification in this framework.

    **Concept Tested:** Income Elasticity of Demand

---

#### 5. Why does demand for "Honeycrisp apples" tend to be more elastic than demand for "apples" as a broad category?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because a narrower category has more close substitutes available, such as other apple varieties
2. Because apples in general are a luxury good while Honeycrisp apples are a necessity
3. Because narrowly defined goods always take up a larger share of a household's budget
4. Because the time horizon for buying a specific apple variety is always longer
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A narrowly defined good like a specific apple variety has many close substitutes (other apple varieties), making its demand more elastic, whereas "apples" as a whole category has fewer substitutes available to someone who specifically wants apples. Option B and C make unsupported claims about necessity and budget share, and option D incorrectly asserts a longer time horizon that is not implied by category width.

    **Concept Tested:** Determinants of Price Elasticity

---

#### 6. The price of Pepsi rises by 10%, and the quantity demanded of Coca-Cola rises by 4%, yielding a cross-price elasticity of 0.4. What does this positive value indicate?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Coke and Pepsi are complements, since their prices move together
2. Coke and Pepsi are substitutes, since a higher price for one increases demand for the other
3. Coke has highly elastic demand with respect to its own price
4. Pepsi's supply curve is upward sloping
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A positive cross-price elasticity means the two goods are substitutes: when Pepsi becomes more expensive, consumers buy more Coke instead. Option A describes the negative cross-price elasticity case (complements), and options C and D both describe own-price or supply relationships that cross-price elasticity does not measure.

    **Concept Tested:** Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand

---

#### 7. A wheat farmer selling into a large, competitive world market faces demand for his own wheat that is best described as:

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Perfectly inelastic, since wheat is a necessity with no substitutes
2. Unit elastic, since wheat prices and quantities move in exact proportion
3. Perfectly elastic, since buyers can switch entirely to other farmers' identical wheat if he raises his price even slightly
4. Highly inelastic, because wheat takes up a small share of most household budgets
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Because the individual farmer's wheat is identical to every other farmer's and sold into a large competitive market, buyers will switch entirely away from him if he raises his price even slightly, making his individual demand curve essentially horizontal, or perfectly elastic. Option A confuses market-level inelasticity with the individual seller's situation, option B misapplies unit elasticity, and option D is also a market-level, not individual-seller, characterization.

    **Concept Tested:** Perfectly Elastic Demand

---

#### 8. When a government imposes a tax on a good, which side of the market bears a larger share of the tax burden?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Whichever side is legally required to send the payment to the government
2. The side with more elastic responsiveness, since it has more ability to avoid the tax
3. The side with more inelastic responsiveness, since it has fewer alternatives and cannot easily avoid the tax by changing quantity
4. Always the sellers, regardless of relative elasticities
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Tax incidence depends on relative elasticities, not on who legally remits the tax: the more inelastic side of the market has fewer substitutes or alternatives and so continues trading nearly the same quantity, absorbing more of the tax through price changes. Option A describes a common misconception the chapter explicitly warns against, option B reverses the correct relationship, and option D ignores that incidence can fall on either side.

    **Concept Tested:** Tax Incidence

#### 9. The price of a monthly parking pass rises from $50 to $55, causing quantity demanded to fall from 8,000 to 7,600 passes. Using the midpoint formula, what is the absolute value of the price elasticity of demand, and is demand elastic or inelastic?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. |E_d| ≈ 1.85; demand is elastic
2. |E_d| ≈ 1.00; demand is unit elastic
3. |E_d| ≈ 0.54; demand is inelastic
4. |E_d| ≈ 0.10; demand is perfectly inelastic
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Using the midpoint formula, $\%\Delta Q = -400/7800 \approx -5.13\%$ and $\%\Delta P = 5/52.5 \approx 9.52\%$, giving $\lvert E_d\rvert \approx 0.54$, which is less than 1, so demand is inelastic. The other options result from arithmetic errors or misclassifying the magnitude relative to 1.

    **Concept Tested:** Price Elasticity of Demand

---

#### 10. Suppose a government taxes gasoline, where demand is highly inelastic ($\lvert E_d\rvert = 0.2$) and supply is relatively elastic ($E_s = 1.5$). Based on relative elasticities, which group bears the larger share of the tax burden?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Producers, because elastic supply means they absorb most price changes
2. Consumers, because inelastic demand means they have fewer alternatives and keep buying nearly the same quantity despite the price increase
3. The burden is always split exactly 50-50 regardless of elasticities
4. Neither group bears the burden; the government absorbs the entire tax through reduced revenue
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Because demand is far more inelastic than supply, consumers have fewer ways to reduce their quantity in response to the tax and end up absorbing most of the price increase, while the relatively elastic supply side can adjust more easily and bears less of the burden. Option A reverses the logic, option C ignores that incidence depends on relative elasticities rather than being fixed at an even split, and option D is incorrect because the tax burden always falls on market participants, not the government itself.

    **Concept Tested:** Tax Incidence
