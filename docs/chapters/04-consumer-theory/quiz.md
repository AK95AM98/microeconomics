# Quiz: Consumer Theory

Test your understanding of utility maximization, indifference curves, budget constraints, and income and substitution effects with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What is marginal utility?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The additional satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good, holding other consumption constant
2. The overall satisfaction a consumer receives from consuming a given quantity of a good
3. The minimum price a consumer would pay to obtain one more unit of a good
4. The rate at which a consumer is willing to trade one good for another while remaining equally satisfied
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Marginal utility is the extra satisfaction gained from one additional unit of a good, calculated as $MU_n = TU_n - TU_{n-1}$. Option B describes total utility, not marginal utility, option C describes willingness to pay rather than a utility measure, and option D describes the marginal rate of substitution.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Utility

---

#### 2. What does an indifference curve represent?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. All combinations of two goods that a consumer can afford given a fixed budget
2. The locus of utility-maximizing bundles across different income levels
3. The relationship between a good's price and the quantity demanded
4. All combinations of two goods that yield the same total utility to the consumer
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. An indifference curve plots every bundle of two goods that leaves the consumer equally satisfied, meaning the consumer is indifferent between any two points on the same curve. Option A describes the budget constraint instead, option B describes a different concept not covered by a single indifference curve, and option C describes a demand curve.

    **Concept Tested:** Indifference Curve

---

#### 3. Which scenario best illustrates the law of diminishing marginal utility?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A consumer's budget line shifts inward after a decrease in income
2. The first slice of pizza provides 20 units of extra satisfaction, the second provides 16, and the third provides 12
3. A consumer substitutes toward a cheaper good when its relative price falls
4. A consumer's total utility turns negative after eating too much food
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Diminishing marginal utility means each additional unit adds *less* satisfaction than the last, exactly as the marginal utility of successive pizza slices falls from 20 to 16 to 12 while still remaining positive. Option A describes a budget constraint shift, option C describes the substitution effect, and option D describes marginal utility turning negative, a distinct and more extreme case than diminishing (but still positive) marginal utility.

    **Concept Tested:** Diminishing Marginal Utility

---

#### 4. Why are indifference curves typically convex (bowed inward) toward the origin?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because marginal utility is always negative for extreme bundles
2. Because the consumer's income constrains how much can be purchased
3. Because the marginal rate of substitution diminishes as the consumer substitutes more of one good for another
4. Because higher indifference curves always represent lower utility
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. As a consumer gives up more and more of one good to get additional units of another, they become less willing to continue the trade, so the marginal rate of substitution declines along the curve, producing its characteristic bowed-in shape. Option A misapplies a feature of extreme bundles, option B describes the budget constraint rather than curve shape, and option D is false — higher indifference curves represent higher, not lower, utility.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Rate of Substitution

---

#### 5. For a normal good, when its price falls, how do the substitution and income effects interact?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Both effects work in the same direction, increasing quantity demanded
2. The substitution effect increases quantity demanded while the income effect decreases it, with the income effect dominating
3. Both effects decrease quantity demanded, reinforcing the law of demand
4. The two effects exactly cancel out, leaving quantity demanded unchanged
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. For a normal good, a lower price makes the good relatively cheaper (substitution effect, quantity up) and raises real purchasing power, which also increases quantity demanded for a normal good (income effect, quantity up) — both effects reinforce each other. Option B describes an inferior good instead, option C reverses the direction of both effects, and option D describes effects that do not actually offset for a normal good.

    **Concept Tested:** Income Effect

#### 6. A consumer's total utility from cups of tea is: 0 cups = 0, 1 cup = 25, 2 cups = 45, 3 cups = 60, 4 cups = 70. What is the marginal utility of the 3rd cup?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 10
2. 20
3. 60
4. 15
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Marginal utility of the 3rd cup is $TU_3 - TU_2 = 60 - 45 = 15$. Option A is actually the marginal utility of the 4th cup, option B is the marginal utility of the 2nd cup, and option C mistakenly reports the total utility level at 3 cups rather than the marginal (additional) utility.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Utility

#### 7. A consumer's utility function is $U(X,Y) = XY$, with income $150, $P_x = \$5$, and $P_y = \$10$. Using the tangency condition and budget constraint, what is the utility-maximizing quantity of $X$?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 10
2. 30
3. 15
4. 20
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. For $U=XY$, $MU_x = Y$ and $MU_y = X$, so the tangency condition gives $Y/X = P_x/P_y = 5/10 = 0.5$, meaning $Y = 0.5X$. Substituting into the budget constraint $5X + 10Y = 150$ gives $5X + 5X = 150$, so $X^* = 15$ (and $Y^* = 7.5$). The other options come from misapplying the price ratio or arithmetic slips in solving the budget equation.

    **Concept Tested:** Tangency Condition

---

#### 8. A consumer has income of $240 to spend on movies ($P_x = \$6$) and books ($P_y = \$8$). What is the maximum number of movies the consumer could buy if all income were spent on movies alone?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 30
2. 32
3. 48
4. 40
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The horizontal intercept of the budget line is found by setting spending on books to zero: $6X = 240 \implies X = 40$ movies. Option A results from dividing income by the wrong price, option B does not correspond to either intercept, and option C uses the price of books instead of movies.

    **Concept Tested:** Budget Constraint

#### 9. A very poor household spends most of its income on rice, a dietary staple. When the price of rice falls, the household's real income effectively rises so much that it shifts spending toward more varied, preferred foods, actually buying *less* rice overall despite the price decline. This behavior is consistent with rice being a:

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Normal good, since quantity demanded responds positively to income changes
2. Giffen good, an extreme inferior good in which the income effect dominates and overwhelms the substitution effect
3. Perfect substitute for other staple foods
4. Good with perfectly elastic demand
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. A Giffen good is defined by quantity demanded falling when price falls, because the negative income effect of an extreme inferior good outweighs the normal substitution effect — exactly the pattern described for the household's rice consumption. Option A describes ordinary normal-good behavior, which this scenario contradicts, and options C and D introduce unrelated classifications not implied by the scenario.

    **Concept Tested:** Giffen Good

---

#### 10. The price of store-brand pasta (an inferior good) falls. The substitution effect alone would increase quantity demanded by 10 units, while the income effect decreases quantity demanded by 4 units. What is the net change in quantity demanded, and does this outcome violate the law of demand?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Quantity demanded falls by 14 units overall, violating the law of demand
2. Quantity demanded rises by 6 units overall, consistent with the law of demand since the substitution effect dominates
3. Quantity demanded is unchanged, since the two effects exactly offset
4. Quantity demanded rises by 14 units overall, since both effects add together
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The substitution effect (+10) and income effect (−4) move in opposite directions for this inferior good, but the substitution effect is larger, so quantity demanded rises by a net 6 units — the law of demand still holds because the good is an ordinary inferior good, not a Giffen good. Option A would require the income effect to dominate, option C incorrectly assumes the effects are equal in size, and option D wrongly adds the effects as if they pointed the same direction.

    **Concept Tested:** Substitution Effect
