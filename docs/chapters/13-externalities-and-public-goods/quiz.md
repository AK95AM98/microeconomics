# Quiz: Externalities and Public Goods

Test your understanding of Pigouvian taxes and subsidies, the Coase theorem, cap-and-trade, the Samuelson condition, and the free-rider problem with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What is "social marginal cost" (SMC)?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Private marginal cost plus the marginal external cost imposed on third parties
2. The private marginal cost a firm pays, ignoring any effects on third parties
3. The marginal cost of abatement under a cap-and-trade system
4. The total cost of producing the first unit of a good
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Social marginal cost equals private marginal cost plus the marginal external cost (SMC = PMC + MEC), capturing the full cost to society of producing one more unit, including the portion a firm does not itself pay. Option B describes private marginal cost alone, option C describes marginal abatement cost, a related but distinct concept, and option D describes an unrelated total-cost measure.

    **Concept Tested:** Social Marginal Cost

---

#### 2. What does the Coase theorem state?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Externalities can only be resolved through government-imposed Pigouvian taxes
2. The efficient outcome always requires the party causing the externality to hold the legal right
3. If property rights are clearly defined, transaction costs are low, and bargaining is possible, private parties can negotiate their way to the efficient outcome regardless of who initially holds the legal right
4. Private bargaining can never achieve an efficient outcome when externalities are present
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The Coase theorem holds that, under low transaction costs and clear property rights, private bargaining reaches the efficient outcome no matter who initially holds the legal right — the assignment of rights only changes who pays whom. Option A contradicts the entire point of the theorem, which is that government taxation is not the only path to efficiency, option B is false since the factory-and-resident example shows efficiency holds regardless of which side holds the right, and option D directly contradicts the theorem's central claim.

    **Concept Tested:** Coase Theorem

---

#### 3. What is the "Samuelson condition" for efficient public goods provision?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Each individual's marginal benefit must equal marginal cost separately
2. The good must be funded entirely through voluntary private contributions
3. Marginal cost must equal zero for the good to be efficiently provided
4. The sum of every individual's marginal benefit must equal the marginal cost of providing the good
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Because a public good is non-rival, everyone who values it consumes the same unit simultaneously, so efficiency requires summing (vertically adding) every individual's marginal benefit and setting that sum equal to marginal cost, rather than equating each person's benefit to cost separately. Option A describes the private-good efficiency condition instead, option B describes a funding mechanism, not an efficiency condition, and option C imposes an unrelated and unsupported requirement.

    **Concept Tested:** Samuelson Condition

---

#### 4. Why does an unregulated market with a negative externality produce more than the efficient quantity?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because firms set price equal to social marginal cost, which is too low
2. Because social marginal cost exceeds private marginal cost, but firms only equate private marginal cost to private marginal benefit, ignoring the external cost
3. Because the government always subsidizes polluting industries
4. Because demand for the externality-generating good is always perfectly inelastic
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Firms base their output decision on private marginal cost, which is lower than the true social marginal cost once the external cost is included, so the market settles at a quantity higher than the efficient one where social marginal cost equals social marginal benefit. Option A incorrectly claims firms use social marginal cost at all, option C invents an unrelated and unsupported subsidy claim, and option D makes an unnecessary and generally false assumption about elasticity.

    **Concept Tested:** Social Marginal Cost

#### 5. Why does a cap-and-trade system achieve a given pollution-reduction target at the lowest total cost?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because trading continues until every firm's marginal abatement cost is equalized to the market permit price, so low-cost abaters do more abatement and high-cost abaters do less
2. Because it requires every firm to reduce emissions by exactly the same percentage
3. Because the government sets a different price for each individual firm
4. Because cap-and-trade eliminates the need for any firm to reduce emissions at all
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Permit trading lets firms with low abatement costs sell their spare permits (by cutting more pollution themselves) to firms with high abatement costs, continuing until every firm's marginal abatement cost equals the same market permit price, which minimizes total abatement cost for the overall target. Option B describes a uniform-cut requirement that the chapter explicitly shows is more costly than trading, option C contradicts the single market-clearing price that trading produces, and option D ignores that the cap still requires real abatement overall.

    **Concept Tested:** Cap-and-Trade

#### 6. Why does the free-rider problem resemble a prisoner's dilemma?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because public goods are always funded successfully through voluntary contributions
2. Because free riding is illegal and punishable under antitrust law
3. Because each individual's dominant strategy is to free ride regardless of what others do, even though mutual contribution would leave everyone better off
4. Because only one person is ever willing to contribute to a public good
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. In the streetlight example, free riding is each neighbor's dominant strategy regardless of the other's choice, yet the resulting (Free Ride, Free Ride) equilibrium leaves both worse off than if both had contributed — exactly the structure of a prisoner's dilemma. Option A contradicts the chapter's central point that voluntary provision tends to underprovide public goods, option B invents an unrelated legal consequence, and option D makes an unsupported generalization about the number of contributors.

    **Concept Tested:** Free-Rider Problem

#### 7. A firm's private marginal cost is $PMC = 4Q$, and demand is $P = 120 - 2Q$. Each unit produced imposes a constant marginal external cost of $30. What is the optimal Pigouvian tax, and what is the resulting efficient quantity?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $t^*=\$15$; $Q=20$
2. $t^*=\$30$; $Q=20$
3. $t^*=\$60$; $Q=10$
4. $t^*=\$30$; $Q=15$
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The optimal Pigouvian tax equals the constant marginal external cost, $t^*=\$30$; with the tax, firms face $PMC+t=4Q+30$, and setting this equal to demand gives $4Q+30=120-2Q \implies 6Q=90 \implies Q=15$, the efficient quantity. Option A and B both misstate the tax or quantity, and option C results from doubling the tax and misreading the resulting quantity.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal External Cost

#### 8. Three households value a shared community garden at $MB_1=50-Q$, $MB_2=30-Q$, and $MB_3=10-Q$, and the marginal cost of expanding the garden is $MC=3Q$. What is the efficient size of the garden according to the Samuelson condition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 10
2. 15
3. 20
4. 30
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Summing marginal benefits vertically gives $\sum MB_i = 90 - 3Q$; setting this equal to marginal cost, $90-3Q=3Q \implies 90=6Q \implies Q^*=15$. The other options result from using only one or two households' marginal benefits, or from an arithmetic slip in solving for $Q$.

    **Concept Tested:** Samuelson Condition

#### 9. Two firms each currently emit 100 tons of pollution. Firm A's marginal abatement cost is $MAC_A=3x$, and Firm B's is $MAC_B=6x$. A regulator requires 60 tons of total abatement. What is the cost-minimizing allocation of abatement between the two firms?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Firm A abates 40 tons, Firm B abates 20 tons
2. Firm A abates 30 tons, Firm B abates 30 tons
3. Firm A abates 20 tons, Firm B abates 40 tons
4. Firm A abates 60 tons, Firm B abates 0 tons
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Cost minimization requires $MAC_A = MAC_B$, so $3x_A = 6x_B \implies x_A = 2x_B$; combined with $x_A + x_B = 60$, this gives $3x_B = 60 \implies x_B=20, x_A=40$. Option B imposes an equal split that does not equalize marginal costs, option C reverses which firm should abate more, and option D asks the cheaper abater to do all the work, which is more abatement than cost minimization requires from Firm A alone.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Abatement Cost

#### 10. Five residents have preferred park budgets of $5,000, $20,000, $40,000, $65,000, and $150,000. Under the median voter theorem, what outcome would majority voting on this single issue produce, and why might this outcome still be inefficient relative to the Samuelson condition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The outcome would always exactly equal the Samuelson-efficient quantity, since voting aggregates everyone's preferences
2. The outcome would be $40,000 (the median), but this may not equal the surplus-maximizing quantity since voting does not weight preferences by intensity, unlike the Samuelson condition's summed marginal benefits
3. The outcome would be $150,000, since the wealthiest voter's preference dominates under majority rule
4. The outcome would be $56,000 (the mean), since majority voting always reflects the average preference
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The median voter theorem predicts the outcome matches the median preference ($40,000, the middle value among the five), but majority voting gives each voter one vote regardless of how intensely they feel, so it generally will not reproduce the Samuelson-efficient quantity, which instead sums everyone's marginal benefit. Option A overstates the theorem's guarantee of efficiency, and options C and D both misidentify the predicted outcome, which is the median, not the maximum or the mean.

    **Concept Tested:** Median Voter Theorem
