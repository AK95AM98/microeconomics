# Quiz: Perfect Competition

Test your understanding of price-taking behavior, the MR = MC rule, the shutdown decision, long-run equilibrium, and allocative efficiency with these review questions.

---

#### 1. Which combination of characteristics defines a perfectly competitive market?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Many buyers and sellers, a standardized product, free entry and exit, and perfect information
2. A single seller, a unique product, high barriers to entry, and imperfect information
3. A few large interdependent sellers producing differentiated products
4. Many sellers producing differentiated products with some control over price
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Perfect competition requires many small buyers and sellers, a homogeneous (standardized) product, free entry and exit, and perfect information. Option B describes a monopoly, option C describes an oligopoly, and option D describes monopolistic competition — all distinct market structures covered in later chapters.

    **Concept Tested:** Perfect Competition

---

#### 2. Why is a competitive firm described as a "price taker"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because it sets its own price independently of the market
2. Because it can influence the market price by changing its output
3. Because its product is sufficiently differentiated that it has some pricing power
4. Because it is one of many sellers of an identical product and has no ability to influence the market price
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. A competitive firm sells an identical product alongside many other sellers, so attempting to charge above the market price would simply drive buyers to competitors, and there is no reason to charge below it. Option A and B describe firms with market power, which a price taker lacks, and option C contradicts the standardized-product condition of perfect competition.

    **Concept Tested:** Price Taker

---

#### 3. How does economic profit differ from accounting profit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Economic profit ignores implicit opportunity costs, while accounting profit includes them
2. Economic profit subtracts all costs, including the implicit opportunity cost of owner-supplied resources, while accounting profit subtracts only explicit costs
3. Economic profit and accounting profit are always equal in competitive markets
4. Economic profit is calculated only in the long run, while accounting profit is calculated only in the short run
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Economic profit subtracts both explicit costs and implicit opportunity costs (such as forgone wages) from revenue, while accounting profit subtracts only explicit, out-of-pocket costs, so a firm can show positive accounting profit while earning zero or negative economic profit. Option A reverses the two definitions, option C is false since the two measures differ whenever implicit costs exist, and option D incorrectly ties the two profit measures to different time horizons.

    **Concept Tested:** Economic Profit

---

#### 4. Why does marginal revenue equal price ($MR = P$) for a perfectly competitive firm specifically?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because the firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve like the overall market
2. Because the firm must lower its price to sell additional units
3. Because the firm can sell every additional unit at the same market price, so each unit adds exactly that price to total revenue
4. Because marginal cost always equals price in competitive markets
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Because a price-taking firm faces a horizontal (perfectly elastic) demand curve at the market price, every additional unit sold adds exactly that price to total revenue, so $MR = P$. Option A and B describe a firm with market power that must lower price to sell more, the opposite of a price taker, and option D confuses the profit-maximizing condition ($P=MC$ at the optimum) with a general identity that holds at every quantity.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Revenue

---

#### 5. According to the shutdown rule, why should a firm continue producing in the short run even while suffering an overall loss, as long as price exceeds average variable cost?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because producing lets the firm cover all variable costs and contribute something toward its unavoidable fixed costs, which must be paid even at zero output
2. Because producing at a loss always eventually becomes profitable in the short run
3. Because fixed costs disappear once a firm continues to produce
4. Because shutting down permanently closes the business, which is always worse than any loss
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Fixed costs must be paid whether or not the firm produces, so if price covers average variable cost and leaves something left over, producing loses less than shutting down and losing the entire fixed cost. Option B makes an unsupported guarantee about future profitability, option C is false since fixed costs are paid regardless of the production decision, and option D confuses a short-run shutdown (temporarily producing zero) with permanently exiting the industry.

    **Concept Tested:** Shutdown Rule

---

#### 6. In the long run, why is economic profit driven to zero in a competitive industry?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because government regulation requires firms to break even
2. Because marginal cost rises indefinitely until it equals average fixed cost
3. Because consumer demand always falls whenever firms try to raise prices
4. Because positive profits attract new entrants, increasing supply and lowering price, while losses cause exits, raising price, until profit reaches zero
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Free entry and exit means positive economic profit draws in new firms, shifting supply right and lowering price, while losses push firms out, shifting supply left and raising price — both processes continue until price settles at $ATC_{\min}$ and profit is zero. Option A invents a regulatory requirement not part of the competitive model, option B misapplies a cost relationship, and option C is unrelated to the entry-and-exit mechanism.

    **Concept Tested:** Long-Run Competitive Equilibrium

---

#### 7. A competitive firm faces a market price of $30. Its marginal cost at each output level is: $Q=1$, $MC=\$12$; $Q=2$, $MC=\$18$; $Q=3$, $MC=\$24$; $Q=4$, $MC=\$30$; $Q=5$, $MC=\$36$; $Q=6$, $MC=\$42$. What quantity maximizes the firm's profit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 3
2. 4
3. 5
4. 6
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The firm should produce where $MC$ last equals or is below $MR=P=\$30$; this occurs exactly at $Q=4$, where $MC=\$30=MR$. Producing the 5th unit would cost $MC=\$36 > MR=\$30$, reducing profit, so option C goes too far, option A stops one unit short of the optimum, leaving profitable units unproduced, and option D goes two units past the optimum.

    **Concept Tested:** MR Equals MC Rule

#### 8. A firm's average total cost is $22 and its average variable cost is $16 at its current output of 8 units, while the market price is $14. According to the shutdown rule, what should the firm do?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Produce, since price exceeds average fixed cost
2. Produce, since price exceeds average total cost
3. Shut down, since price is below average variable cost, so the firm cannot cover its variable costs
4. Shut down, since price exceeds average variable cost
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Because the market price of $14 is below average variable cost of $16, the firm cannot even cover its variable costs by producing, so it minimizes losses by shutting down and losing only its fixed cost. Option A misapplies a comparison to average fixed cost rather than average variable cost, option B is factually wrong since price ($14) is actually below average total cost ($22), not above it, and option D states the shutdown decision but gives an incorrect, reversed reason.

    **Concept Tested:** Shutdown Rule

---

#### 9. Every firm in a competitive industry has an $ATC_{\min}$ of $15 per unit at an output of 80 units per firm. At the long-run equilibrium price of $15, market demand is 60,000 units. How many firms will operate in the industry in long-run equilibrium?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 750
2. 600
3. 800
4. 500
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The number of firms equals market quantity demanded divided by each firm's output: $60{,}000 / 80 = 750$ firms. The other options result from dividing by an incorrect per-firm output figure or misreading the given quantities.

    **Concept Tested:** Long-Run Competitive Equilibrium

---

#### 10. A price ceiling forces the market price for a good below its competitive equilibrium level, where price originally equaled marginal cost. What happens to allocative efficiency and total surplus as a result?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Allocative efficiency improves, since lower prices always benefit consumers
2. Total surplus rises, since consumer surplus increases by exactly as much as producer surplus falls
3. A deadweight loss arises, because some units where consumers' willingness to pay exceeds the marginal cost of production are no longer produced and exchanged
4. There is no effect on efficiency, since price controls only redistribute surplus between buyers and sellers without changing total surplus
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Forcing price below the point where $P=MC$ reduces the quantity firms are willing to supply, so some mutually beneficial trades — where buyers would pay more than the cost of production — no longer occur, creating a deadweight loss and breaking allocative efficiency. Option A incorrectly assumes lower prices are always efficient regardless of quantity effects, and options B and D both wrongly claim total surplus is unaffected, when in fact the lost trades reduce it.

    **Concept Tested:** Allocative Efficiency
