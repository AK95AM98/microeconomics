# Quiz: Factor Markets

Test your understanding of derived demand, marginal revenue product, labor supply, monopsony, and capital and land markets with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What does it mean that the demand for labor is a "derived demand"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Labor demand is determined solely by government wage-setting policy
2. Workers derive utility directly from the act of working itself
3. Labor demand is unrelated to the market for the final good being produced
4. The demand for labor arises from, and depends on, the demand for the output that labor helps produce
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Nobody hires a worker for the worker's own sake; firms demand labor because it helps produce an output they can sell, so the demand for labor is derived from the demand for that output. Option A invents a government role not part of the concept, option B misattributes the firm's motivation to the worker's own utility, and option C directly contradicts the defining feature of derived demand.

    **Concept Tested:** Derived Demand

---

#### 2. How is marginal revenue product (MRP) calculated for a competitive firm?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. MRP equals the marginal product of labor multiplied by the market price of output
2. MRP equals the wage rate divided by the marginal product of labor
3. MRP equals average product multiplied by total revenue
4. MRP equals total revenue divided by the number of workers employed
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Since $MRP_L = MP_L \times MR$, and $MR = P$ for a competitive firm, $MRP_L = MP_L \times P$ — the extra output from one more worker valued at the market price. Option B inverts the relationship, option C confuses average product with marginal product and misuses total revenue, and option D describes an unrelated average measure rather than a marginal one.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Revenue Product

---

#### 3. What is "economic rent"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The monthly payment a renter makes for an apartment
2. The additional cost a monopsonist incurs from hiring one more worker
3. A payment to a factor of production whose supply is fixed, determined entirely by demand since supply cannot respond
4. The portion of a worker's wage attributable to human capital investment
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Economic rent is the payment to a factor like land whose supply is essentially fixed; because supply cannot respond to price, the entire market-clearing payment is determined by demand alone and counts as rent. Option A describes an everyday use of the word "rent" unrelated to the economic concept, option B describes marginal cost of labor instead, and option D describes a return to human capital investment, a distinct concept from rent on a fixed-supply factor.

    **Concept Tested:** Economic Rent

---

#### 4. According to the hiring rule for a profit-maximizing firm, why should a firm stop hiring additional workers once MRP falls below the wage?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because at that point, the next worker would cost more than the additional revenue they generate, reducing profit
2. Because labor laws require firms to stop hiring once a certain wage threshold is reached
3. Because marginal product always becomes negative once MRP falls below the wage
4. Because firms must always hire the same number of workers as their competitors
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. The hiring rule, $MRP_L = w$, is a marginal-benefit-equals-marginal-cost condition: once $MRP_L < w$, hiring that worker would cost more (the wage) than it adds in revenue (MRP), reducing profit. Option B invents a legal requirement not part of the economic model, option C is false since marginal product can still be positive, just low, when MRP falls below the wage, and option D is an unrelated and unsupported claim about competitor behavior.

    **Concept Tested:** Labor Hiring Rule

---

#### 5. Why can an individual's labor supply curve bend backward at high wage levels?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because the substitution effect always dominates at every wage level
2. Because firms refuse to pay wages above a certain threshold
3. Because higher wages always reduce a worker's marginal product
4. Because past some wage level, the income effect of a higher wage (wanting more leisure as a normal good) can dominate the substitution effect (wanting to work more because leisure is now more costly), leading to fewer hours worked
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. At high wages, a worker may be rich enough that the income effect — wanting more leisure as income rises — outweighs the substitution effect of a higher opportunity cost of leisure, causing hours worked to fall even as the wage continues to rise. Option A describes the opposite of what produces a backward bend, option B introduces an unrelated firm-side constraint, and option C confuses the wage-setting mechanism with a worker's own productivity, which this model does not assume changes with the wage.

    **Concept Tested:** Backward-Bending Labor Supply

#### 6. Compared to a competitive labor market, how does monopsony affect wages and employment?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Monopsony raises both wages and employment above the competitive level
2. Monopsony has no effect on wages or employment, since firms still face the same supply curve
3. Monopsony results in a lower wage and fewer workers hired than in a competitive labor market
4. Monopsony only affects employment, leaving the wage unchanged
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Because a monopsonist's marginal cost of labor exceeds the wage, it hires where $MRP_L = MCL$ and then pays only the (lower) wage read off the supply curve, resulting in both fewer workers hired and a lower wage than the competitive outcome. Option A reverses the actual direction of the effect, option B ignores that the monopsonist, as the sole buyer, faces the upward-sloping supply curve directly rather than a flat market wage, and option D incorrectly claims only one variable is affected when both wage and employment fall.

    **Concept Tested:** Monopsony

---

#### 7. A firm sells output at a competitive price of $8. The marginal product of the 5th worker is 15 units, the 6th worker is 10 units, and the 7th worker is 5 units. If the market wage is $70 per week, how many of these three additional workers (5th, 6th, and 7th) should the firm hire?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. All three workers (5th, 6th, and 7th)
2. Only the 5th and 6th workers
3. Only the 5th worker
4. None of the three workers
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. $MRP_5 = 15\times\$8=\$120$, $MRP_6=10\times\$8=\$80$, and $MRP_7=5\times\$8=\$40$; the firm hires any worker whose MRP is at least the $70 wage, so it hires the 5th ($120\geq70$) and 6th ($80\geq70$), but not the 7th ($40<70$). Option A incorrectly includes the unprofitable 7th worker, option C stops one worker short of the profitable hires, and option D wrongly excludes workers whose MRP clearly exceeds the wage.

    **Concept Tested:** Marginal Revenue Product

#### 8. The labor market for dental hygienists is described by $L^d = 3000 - 50w$ and $L^s = 600 + 100w$. What is the equilibrium wage?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $12
2. $14
3. $16
4. $18
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Setting $L^d=L^s$ gives $3000-50w=600+100w \implies 2400=150w \implies w^*=\$16$. The other options result from arithmetic slips when combining the coefficients or constants on each side of the equation.

    **Concept Tested:** Labor Market Equilibrium

#### 9. A firm can buy a machine today for $20,000 that will generate a single payoff of $23,000 one year from now. If the interest rate is 12%, what is the present value of the payoff, and should the firm buy the machine?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. PV ≈ $20,536; buy the machine, since PV exceeds its cost
2. PV ≈ $25,760; do not buy the machine, since PV exceeds its cost
3. PV ≈ $18,500; do not buy the machine, since PV is below its cost
4. PV ≈ $23,000; buy the machine, since PV equals its future payoff
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. $PV = \dfrac{23{,}000}{1.12} \approx \$20{,}536$, which exceeds the $20,000 cost, so the machine has a positive net present value and the firm should buy it. Option B multiplies instead of discounting, option C understates the present value and also contradicts itself by rejecting a project whose PV it claims exceeds cost, and option D incorrectly treats present value as equal to the undiscounted future payoff.

    **Concept Tested:** Present Value

#### 10. In a monopsony labor market, a policymaker proposes a minimum wage set between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage. Based on the chapter's analysis, what is the likely effect of this policy?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. It would reduce both the wage and employment further below the monopsony outcome
2. It would increase both the wage and employment, moving the market closer to the competitive outcome, unlike the typical effect of a minimum wage in a competitive labor market
3. It would have no effect, since monopsonists always pay the competitive wage already
4. It would increase the wage but necessarily create unemployment, exactly as a minimum wage would in a competitive labor market
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. In a pure monopsony model, a minimum wage set in this range forces the monopsonist closer to the competitive hiring rule, which can raise both the wage and employment simultaneously — a result that would not occur under a minimum wage imposed on an already-competitive labor market. Option A describes the opposite of the documented effect, option C is false since a monopsonist's wage is below the competitive level by definition, and option D incorrectly imports the competitive-market logic (where a binding minimum wage creates a surplus of workers) into the monopsony case, where it does not apply in the same way.

    **Concept Tested:** Monopsony Hiring Rule
