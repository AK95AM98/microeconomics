# Quiz: Monopolistic Competition

Test your understanding of product differentiation, short-run and long-run equilibrium, excess capacity, and non-price competition with these review questions.

---

#### 1. What combination of features defines monopolistic competition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. One seller, no close substitutes, and high barriers to entry
2. Many firms, differentiated products, and free entry and exit
3. A few large firms whose output decisions depend on each other
4. Many firms selling an identical, standardized product with no pricing power
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Monopolistic competition combines many firms and free entry and exit, like perfect competition, with differentiated products that give each firm some pricing power, unlike perfect competition. Option A describes monopoly, option C describes oligopoly, and option D describes perfect competition.

    **Concept Tested:** Monopolistic Competition

---

#### 2. What is "product differentiation"?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The practice of selling an identical product at the lowest possible price
2. The process by which entry drives economic profit to zero
3. A measure of how concentrated a market is among a few large firms
4. A firm's effort to make its product distinct from competitors' in the eyes of consumers
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Product differentiation is how a firm distinguishes its product from rivals' through features, location, service, or branding, giving it a downward-sloping demand curve rather than a horizontal one. Option A describes competing purely on price for an identical good, option B describes the long-run entry mechanism, and option C describes a concentration measure unrelated to differentiation itself.

    **Concept Tested:** Product Differentiation

---

#### 3. What is "excess capacity" in monopolistic competition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The gap between a firm's actual long-run output and the output level that would minimize its average total cost
2. The amount by which a firm's price exceeds its marginal cost
3. The additional profit a firm earns from brand loyalty
4. The portion of market demand not served by any firm
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Because a downward-sloping demand curve can only be tangent to a U-shaped ATC curve on its declining portion, each firm produces less than the quantity that would minimize its average total cost — this shortfall is excess capacity. Option B describes the markup, a related but distinct concept, option C is not a defined measure in this framework, and option D describes unmet market demand rather than a single firm's output shortfall.

    **Concept Tested:** Excess Capacity

---

#### 4. Why is long-run economic profit driven to zero in monopolistic competition, just as in perfect competition?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because government regulation requires firms to break even
2. Because advertising expenditures always equal any profit earned
3. Because monopolistically competitive firms are legally required to price at marginal cost
4. Because free entry allows new firms to enter whenever existing firms earn positive economic profit, shifting each incumbent's demand curve left until profit disappears
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Positive short-run profit attracts new entrants offering their own differentiated versions of the product, which shrinks and flattens each incumbent's demand curve until price just equals average total cost at the best quantity. Option A invents a regulatory requirement that does not exist in this model, option B incorrectly ties advertising spending mechanically to profit, and option C is false since monopolistically competitive firms actually price above marginal cost, not at it.

    **Concept Tested:** Monopolistic Zero Profit

---

#### 5. Why does a monopolistically competitive firm charge a price above marginal cost even in long-run equilibrium?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Because its downward-sloping demand curve means marginal revenue lies below price, so setting $MR = MC$ leaves price above $MC$
2. Because the firm faces no competition at all
3. Because average total cost is always below marginal cost in the long run
4. Because entry has been completely blocked in this market structure
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Since the firm's demand curve slopes downward, marginal revenue lies below price at every quantity; setting the profit-maximizing condition $MR=MC$ therefore necessarily leaves $P > MC$. Option B is false because monopolistic competition features many competing firms, option C makes an unsupported and generally false cost claim, and option D contradicts the free-entry condition that defines this market structure.

    **Concept Tested:** Markup Over Marginal Cost

---

#### 6. Which statement best represents the "persuasive" view of advertising?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Advertising lowers consumers' search costs by providing useful facts about price and availability
2. Advertising always lowers a firm's average costs by increasing sales volume
3. Advertising manipulates consumer preferences and builds artificial brand loyalty without conveying real product information
4. Advertising has no measurable effect on consumer behavior
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The persuasive view holds that advertising shapes preferences and builds brand loyalty unrelated to genuine product differences, raising barriers to entry since rivals must also spend heavily to compete. Option A describes the informative view instead, option B overstates a possible but not guaranteed cost effect, and option D contradicts the entire premise of the debate over advertising's effects.

    **Concept Tested:** Persuasive Advertising

---

#### 7. A local bakery faces demand $P = 10 - 0.01Q$ and has marginal cost $MC = 4$, with fixed costs of $300 (so $TC = 300 + 4Q$). What are the profit-maximizing quantity and price?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $Q=250$, $P=\$6$
2. $Q=200$, $P=\$8$
3. $Q=300$, $P=\$7$
4. $Q=350$, $P=\$6.50$
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Marginal revenue is $MR = 10 - 0.02Q$; setting $MR=MC$ gives $10-0.02Q=4 \implies Q=300$, and price from the demand curve is $P = 10 - 0.01(300) = \$7$. The other options result from arithmetic slips in solving for $Q$ or from reading the wrong point off the demand curve.

    **Concept Tested:** Monopolistic Short-Run Profit

---

#### 8. Using the bakery's numbers from the previous question, what is its short-run economic profit?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. $600
2. $300
3. $900
4. $1,500
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Total cost at $Q=300$ is $TC = 300 + 4(300) = \$1{,}500$, so $ATC = 1{,}500/300 = \$5$; profit is $(P-ATC)\times Q = (7-5)\times 300 = \$600$. Option B is the fixed cost alone, option C overstates the markup per unit, and option D is simply the total cost figure rather than the profit.

    **Concept Tested:** Monopolistic Short-Run Profit

---

#### 9. A firm's $ATC$ curve reaches its minimum value of $6 at $Q=400$ units. In long-run equilibrium, the firm produces 280 units, where price equals $ATC$ at $6.80. What is the firm's excess capacity?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. 80 units
2. 160 units
3. 400 units
4. 120 units
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Excess capacity is $Q_{\min ATC} - Q^* = 400 - 280 = 120$ units. Option A and B come from arithmetic slips in the subtraction, and option C mistakenly reports the minimum-ATC quantity itself rather than the gap between the two quantities.

    **Concept Tested:** Excess Capacity

---

#### 10. A policymaker argues that monopolistic competition is inefficient because $P>MC$ and firms operate with excess capacity, and proposes forcing the industry toward fewer, larger, standardized firms to eliminate these inefficiencies. What is the strongest economic counterargument to this proposal?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Monopolistic competition always earns positive long-run economic profit, so efficiency is not actually a concern
2. The efficiency gain from lower average costs would come at the cost of reduced product variety, which consumers may value enough to outweigh the efficiency loss from excess capacity and markups
3. Larger standardized firms would eliminate advertising entirely, which is a goal in itself
4. Excess capacity does not actually exist in real-world monopolistically competitive markets
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter's welfare analysis stresses that the apparent inefficiency of excess capacity and markups must be weighed against the real value consumers place on product variety, which fewer standardized firms would eliminate. Option A is factually wrong since long-run economic profit is zero in monopolistic competition, option C treats eliminating advertising as inherently desirable without justification, and option D denies a well-established feature of the model that the question itself assumes.

    **Concept Tested:** Product Variety
